# Crime Busters — Complete Teaching Knowledge Document (Year 2)

The reference spine for the whole curriculum. Every session script, quiz, and
homework set is built from what's here. Keep this open during practice for
questions that go past the script.

**How to read it:** each chapter has a short **"Year 1 recap"** box (what the team
should already know — recap it fast, don't re-teach) followed by the **year-2
material** (what real invitational tests demand and year 1 never covered). Facts
are grounded in the official Division B rules and in what real invitational exams
have actually asked. Where authoritative sources disagree, the disagreement is
noted rather than papered over.

> **Scope — core vs. invitational stretch.** The official 2025 rules are narrower
> than most invitational tests. Officially the event covers only: the **13 listed
> solids** (anhydrous sodium acetate, yeast, vitamin C, calcium carbonate, table
> salt, sugar, flour, gypsum, cornstarch, baking soda, gelatin, Alka-Seltzer,
> white sand), **6 metals** (Al, Fe, Zn, Mg, Cu, Sn), **6 liquids** (lemon juice,
> isopropyl alcohol, 3% ammonia, water, vinegar, 3% hydrogen peroxide), **7
> plastics** (PETE, HDPE, non-expanded PS, LDPE, PP, PVC, PMMA), **hair** as
> human/dog/cat only, **fibers** as animal/vegetable/synthetic only, **prints** as
> whorl/loop/arch only, and **no calculations** for spatter or tracks.
> Invitationals routinely go further — specific fiber names, the full IAFIS
> 8-pattern set, the spatter-angle calculation, polycarbonate, and off-list
> powders like Epsom salt, cream of tartar, citric acid, MSG, and washing soda.
> This document teaches the invitational-level version and flags which parts are
> "stretch." See `Test_Coverage_Map.md` for the full topic-by-topic breakdown.

## Chapter 1: The Event and How It Is Scored

Crime Busters is a **two-person laboratory event** with about a **50-minute** time
limit. The team is given a crime scenario, a list of suspects, and physical
evidence, and must run tests to identify the evidence and name the culprit.

### The score breakdown (approximate, from the rules)

| Category | Share of score | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Qualitative analysis** | ~50% | identifying unknown solids (powders), liquids, and metals by solubility, acidity, magnetism, color, density, odor, and reaction to reagents |
| **Polymers / natural vs. man-made** | ~10% | human vs. dog vs. cat hair; animal vs. vegetable vs. synthetic fiber; plastic identification |
| **Paper chromatography** | ~5% | analyzing chromatograms (ink, dye, juice) and computing Rf |
| **Crime-scene physical evidence** | ~10% | fingerprints, DNA, shoeprints, tire treads, soil, spatter |
| **Analysis** | ~25% | the written report: which evidence implicates which suspect, and why the others are cleared |

Two lessons from that table:

1. **Qualitative analysis is half the test.** It is also the part year 1 treated
   as a lookup table rather than a taught skill. Most of this curriculum lives in
   Chapters 3–7.
2. **The written analysis is a quarter of the test** and is almost pure
   test-taking skill — it rewards a specific structure. Chapter 18 and the
   practice-test answer-review guide drill it.

### Competition logistics the team must know cold

- **Safety gate:** goggles, apron or lab coat, skin covered neck to toe, long
  hair tied back. A team not dressed for safety is not allowed to compete.
- **Note sheet:** each participant may bring **one 8.5" × 11" sheet**, notes on
  both sides, in a sheet protector. Two people → two sheets. Building these sheets
  is part of the curriculum (see the practice-test guide).
- **Calculator:** a non-programmable calculator (Class II) is allowed. Bring one
  that does `sin⁻¹` (arcsin) — it is needed for spatter angles.
- **The supervisor provides** iodine reagent, 1 M HCl, chromatography materials, a
  waste container, and a wash bottle of distilled water — and sometimes a
  microscope or a candle. The team brings everything else on the event's
  recommended equipment list. Not bringing the recommended kit is a real
  disadvantage.
- **Cleanup is scored.** Penalties up to 10% for leaving a dirty station or
  bringing prohibited equipment. Practice cleanup every single time.

## Chapter 2: Working a Crime Scene: Principles and Evidence Types

> **Year 1 recap:** the team has seen crime scenarios and matched evidence to
> suspects, but the *vocabulary* of forensic reasoning was mostly implicit.

### Locard's Exchange Principle

**"Every contact leaves a trace."** When two objects touch, material transfers
both ways — the criminal leaves something at the scene (hair, fiber, prints,
soil) and carries something away (glass, blood, carpet fiber). Edmond Locard,
early 1900s. This is *why* trace evidence works at all, and it is a common
short-answer question.

### Class vs. individual characteristics

- **Class characteristics** put an item in a group: "a size-9 running shoe with a
  herringbone tread," "cotton fiber," "type A blood," "a loop fingerprint
  pattern." Class evidence can *exclude* a suspect and can *support* a case, but
  cannot by itself prove one person did it.
- **Individual characteristics** point to one source: a specific cut in a shoe
  sole, a fingerprint's full minutiae set, a DNA profile, a tool mark's unique
  striations. 
- Almost everything a Crime Busters team identifies is **class evidence**. The
  case is built by *stacking* class evidence until only one suspect fits all of
  it. Say this in the analysis: "no single item proves it, but Suspect 3 is the
  only person matched by the soil, the fiber, and the powder together."

### Direct vs. circumstantial evidence

- **Direct** evidence proves a fact by itself — an eyewitness, a confession, a
  security video. Crime Busters teams rarely get this.
- **Circumstantial** evidence requires an inference — "her hair was on the
  victim, so she was probably there." A strong case can be built entirely on
  circumstantial evidence when enough of it converges. Do not treat
  "circumstantial" as a synonym for "weak."

### Presumptive vs. confirmatory tests

- A **presumptive** test is fast and sensitive but not specific — it says "this
  *could* be blood / starch / a carbonate." Examples: Kastle-Meyer (blood),
  luminol (blood), iodine (starch), a fizz with acid (carbonate).
- A **confirmatory** test is specific — it identifies the substance with
  certainty. Examples: DNA profiling, a microcrystalline test, spectroscopy.
- Crime Busters is almost entirely **presumptive** testing plus **comparison**.
  The team is not proving "this is definitely cornstarch" in a lab-certified
  sense; it is showing "this behaves exactly like cornstarch and matches Suspect
  2's kitchen."

### Chain of custody

The documented trail of who collected, handled, stored, and analyzed each piece
of evidence, with dates and signatures, from the scene to the courtroom. A broken
chain of custody makes evidence inadmissible. Common one-line question: *"Why is
chain of custody important?"* → so the evidence presented in court is provably
the same evidence collected at the scene, un-tampered.

### The seven-step scene walkthrough (for context)

Secure and isolate → document (notes, photos, sketch) → search systematically →
collect and package (each item separately, sealed, labeled) → maintain chain of
custody → analyze in the lab → reconstruct the event. Crime Busters drops the
team in at "analyze," but knowing the whole arc helps with short-answer
questions.

## Chapter 3: Qualitative Analysis: The Systematic Workflow

> **Year 1 recap:** the team has a powder-ID table and has done some matching.
> What's new: turning that table into a **fast, repeatable procedure** that works
> on powders the table doesn't list, and recording results the way a key expects.

Qualitative analysis = *what is it*, not *how much*. The team's job: for each
unknown, run a short battery of tests, read the results against known behavior,
and name it. Speed comes from always doing the tests in the same order.

### The standard powder battery

Run these in order. Use a **pinch** of sample per test — about what fits on the
tip of a toothpick. Work on a white tray or a labeled spot plate.

| # | Test | How | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Appearance** | look, with a hand lens | crystal (salt, sugar), granular, fine powder (flour, cornstarch), fibrous; color; sheen |
| 2 | **Odor** | waft toward nose | vinegar (sodium acetate when wet), "flour" smell, odorless |
| 3 | **Feel** | rub between fingers (gloved) | gritty (sand, sugar), silky/squeaky (cornstarch), fine and soft (flour, baking soda) |
| 4 | **Solubility in water** | pinch + ~1 mL water, stir | fully dissolves (salt, sugar, Epsom, sodium acetate, citric acid); stays cloudy / suspension (flour, chalk, gypsum); makes a shear-thickening "oobleck" (cornstarch) |
| 5 | **pH of the water mix** | dip pH paper or add cabbage indicator | acidic (citric acid, cream of tartar, vitamin C); near neutral (salt, sugar, cornstarch); basic (baking soda, sodium carbonate, sodium acetate) |
| 6 | **Reaction with acid** (vinegar at home; 1 M HCl at competition) | pinch + a few drops | vigorous fizz = carbonate/bicarbonate (baking soda, chalk, sodium carbonate); fizz only after mixing with a dry acid + water = baking powder / Alka-Seltzer; no fizz = most others |
| 7 | **Reaction with iodine** | one drop of iodine on a dry pinch | blue-black / near-black = starch present (cornstarch, flour, baking powder, powdered sugar); stays orange-brown = no starch; **goes colorless = a reducing agent (vitamin C)** |
| 8 | **Flame / heat behavior** (demo only, coach does it) | tiny amount on foil over heat | sugar melts and caramelizes then chars sweet; starch chars; salt crackles/pops, does not melt; carbonates just sit |

### Recording results the way a key wants them

Real keys give **2 points for the correct name** and **1 point per correctly
implicated suspect**, and often **2 points for the chemical formula**. So the
answer sheet entry for each sample is:

> **Sample 3:** Gypsum — CaSO₄·2H₂O — implicates Suspect 1 (works with drywall)

Write the name *and* the formula *and* the reason, every time. A name with no
reason often loses the implication point.

### Building and using a dichotomous key

A **dichotomous key** is a branching set of either/or questions that leads to an
ID. It's the fastest way to work a bench of 6–8 unknowns because each test splits
the remaining candidates roughly in half.

Example key for a common white-powder set (baking soda, chalk, cornstarch, salt,
sugar, gypsum):

```
1. Fizzes strongly with acid?
   ├─ Yes → 2
   └─ No  → 3
2. Dissolves completely in water?
   ├─ Yes → Baking soda (NaHCO3)
   └─ No  → Calcium carbonate / chalk (CaCO3)
3. Turns blue-black with iodine?
   ├─ Yes → Cornstarch ((C6H10O5)n)
   └─ No  → 4
4. Dissolves completely in water?
   ├─ Yes → 5
   └─ No  → Gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O)
5. Crystals are cubic and it does NOT melt/caramelize on heat?
   ├─ Yes → Table salt (NaCl)
   └─ No  → Sugar / sucrose (C12H22O11)
```

Homework 2 has the team read keys like this and build one for a new set.

### Mixtures

A sample can be **two powders mixed**. Tell-tale signs: two different crystal
shapes or colors under the lens; a partial result (some fizz *and* an
undissolved residue; iodine turns part of the smear black and leaves grains
orange). Keys award points **per component identified**. Work a suspected mixture
by asking "which single powder explains *all* of this?" — if none does, it's a
mixture, and each observation belongs to one component.

### Why the iodine test works (this exact question is asked)

Starch is two glucose polymers: **amylose** (long unbranched chains) and
**amylopectin** (branched). In water, amylose coils into a **helix**. Iodine in
the reagent is actually present as **triiodide** (I₃⁻) and longer polyiodide
ions. Those linear ions slide **inside** the amylose helix and line up end to
end. The trapped iodine and the helix share electrons in a **charge-transfer
complex** that absorbs most visible light, so the mixture looks **blue-black**.
Key points for full marks:

- It is **not a chemical reaction** — no bonds are broken or formed. It is an
  **inclusion complex** (a *clathrate*): one molecule caged inside another.
- **Amylose → blue-black. Amylopectin and glycogen → red-brown** (their branches
  are too short to hold a long iodine chain).
- **Heat reverses it** — the helix uncoils above ~35–50 °C and the color
  disappears, then comes back on cooling.
- **Simple sugars (sucrose, glucose) give no color** — no helix to trap iodine.

### Why carbonates fizz with acid

A carbonate (CO₃²⁻) or bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) reacts with acid to release **carbon
dioxide gas** — that's the fizz.

- Bicarbonate: `HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂↑`
- Carbonate: `CO₃²⁻ + 2 H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂↑`
- Full equation, baking soda + vinegar:
  `NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂↑`

This is both an **acid–base** reaction and, because a gas leaves, it drives to
completion. Chalk (CaCO₃) fizzes the same way but is insoluble, so the reaction
is slower and happens at the grain surface.

### Reducing sugars and the Benedict's test (short-answer only — reagent not supplied)

**Reducing sugars** (glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose) have a free aldehyde or
ketone group that can *reduce* copper(II). Heated with **Benedict's** or
**Fehling's** solution (blue Cu²⁺), they turn it **green → yellow → brick-red**
(Cu₂O precipitate). **Sucrose and starch are non-reducing** and stay blue — unless
first hydrolyzed (broken into their monosaccharides). Expect this as a "what does
it mean if Benedict's turns red" question.

### The flour-specific question

*"Why does flour turn black with iodine?"* → because flour is mostly **starch**,
and the amylose fraction forms the iodine–helix charge-transfer complex described
above. (Flour also contains protein/gluten, but that is not what the iodine
detects.)

## Chapter 5: Liquids and the pH Scale

> **Year 1 recap:** the team has a liquids property table (water, vinegar,
> ammonia, hydrogen peroxide, lemon juice, isopropyl alcohol). What's new:
> **formulas**, the meaning of **pH**, and the acid/base chemistry underneath.

### The reference table

| Liquid | Formula | pH | Odor | Tells |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Water | H₂O | 7 | none | the control; everything else is compared to it |
| Vinegar (dilute acetic acid) | CH₃COOH, also written C₂H₄O₂ | 2–3 | sharp, sour | weak acid; **empirical formula CH₂O** |
| Lemon juice (citric acid soln.) | ~C₆H₈O₇ | 2–3 | citrus | weak acid |
| Household ammonia | NH₃ (aq) → NH₄OH | 11–12 | sharp, pungent | weak base; cleaner |
| Hydrogen peroxide | H₂O₂ | 4–6 | faint | **foams on contact with blood or raw potato** (catalase); bleach/antiseptic |
| Rubbing alcohol (isopropyl) | C₃H₈O (isopropanol) | ~6–7 | medicinal | flammable; evaporates fast; density ~0.79 |
| Ethanol | C₂H₆O | ~7 | alcohol | flammable |
| Saturated salt water | NaCl (aq) | 7 | none | dense (~1.2 g/mL); used in plastic density tests |
| Sugar water | C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (aq) | 7 | faint sweet | denser than water; sticky residue on drying |
| Bleach (sodium hypochlorite) | NaOCl | 11–13 | chlorine | **not usually supplied** — strong; know it for short answer |

### What pH means

**pH stands for "potential (or power) of Hydrogen."** It measures the
**concentration of hydrogen ions, H⁺**, in a solution:

`pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]`

- **Lower pH → more H⁺ → more acidic.** pH 2 has **ten times** more H⁺ than pH 3
  and **a hundred times** more than pH 4. The scale is logarithmic — each whole
  number is a factor of 10.
- pH 7 is **neutral** (pure water): [H⁺] = [OH⁻].
- pH **below 7 is acidic**, pH **above 7 is basic (alkaline)**.
- Standard scale runs 0–14 (it can go slightly outside for very concentrated
  solutions).

Common exact question: *"If a liquid has a small pH value, what does it have more
of?"* → a **higher concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺)**.

### Acids and bases (the level tested)

- **Acid:** donates H⁺ (or, equivalently, produces H₃O⁺ in water). Tastes sour,
  turns litmus red, turns cabbage indicator pink/red, reacts with active metals
  to give H₂ gas, reacts with carbonates to give CO₂.
- **Base:** accepts H⁺ / produces OH⁻. Tastes bitter, feels slippery, turns
  litmus blue, turns cabbage indicator green/yellow.
- **Neutralization:** acid + base → salt + water.
  `HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O`
- **Red-cabbage indicator** (the home kit's pH tool) runs through a color range:
  red (pH 2) → pink (4) → purple (7) → blue (8) → green (10) → yellow (12).

### HCl safety (in-person only)

1 M hydrochloric acid is supplied at competition and used for the metal and
carbonate tests. It **is** a strong acid — it will burn skin and eyes and ruin
clothes. Rules for the team: goggles down, gloves on, work over the tray, a few
**drops** only, cap the bottle between uses, rinse any splash with lots of water
and tell the supervisor. This is practiced only at in-person practice with the
coach. At home, **vinegar is the stand-in and it is enough.**

## Chapter 6: Metals

> **Year 1 recap:** the team saw the metals list and ranked reactivity to acid on
> a homework sheet. What's new: **density calculation, atomic number, reaction
> types, oxide layers, alloys**, and using reaction *vigor* as an ID axis.

### The recommended metals

| Metal | Symbol | Atomic # | Density (g/cm³) | Color | Magnetic? | Reaction with HCl |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | Mg | 12 | 1.74 | dull silver-white | no | vigorous fizz, fast |
| Aluminum | Al | 13 | 2.70 | silvery | no | slow to start (oxide layer), then steady |
| Iron | Fe | 26 | 7.87 | gray, may show rust | **yes** | slow–moderate fizz; solution turns pale green |
| Zinc | Zn | 30 | 7.14 | bluish-gray | no | moderate, steady fizz |
| Tin | Sn | 50 | 7.27 (white tin) | silvery, soft | no | very slow / barely reacts |
| Copper | Cu | 29 | 8.96 | **reddish-orange** | no | **no reaction** with HCl |

Identification axes, in the order they're usually decisive:

1. **Color** — copper is unmistakable (reddish). Everything else is gray/silver.
2. **Magnetism** — only **iron** on this list is attracted to a magnet
   (nickel and cobalt are too, but aren't usually in the set).
3. **Density** — weigh it, measure volume by water displacement, divide.
   Magnesium (1.74) and aluminum (2.70) are much lighter than the rest.
4. **Reaction with HCl** — vigor ranks Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Sn > Cu (none). This
   is the **activity series** in miniature.
5. **Hardness / feel** — tin and aluminum are soft; iron is hard.

### Flame test (may be provided or performed)

Heating a metal or a metal salt in a flame can give a characteristic color as
electrons absorb energy and release it as light. The supervisor may provide a
candle, and some invitationals give the flame color as a clue.

| Element | Flame color |
|---|---|
| **Copper** | **green / blue-green** |
| Sodium (or any Na⁺ contamination, incl. table salt) | intense **yellow-orange** |
| Potassium | lilac / pale violet |
| Calcium | orange-red (brick) |
| Lithium, strontium | red / crimson |
| Barium | pale/yellow-green |
| Boron | bright green |

Of the six Crime Busters metals, **only copper's green flame is clearly
diagnostic.** Magnesium, aluminum, zinc, tin, and iron give a weak or white-ish
flame with no distinctive color, so you separate those by density, magnetism
(iron), and acid vigor. Beware: a trace of salt on your hands or tongs turns any
flame yellow — that's sodium, not the sample.

### Density calculation (directly tested)

`density = mass ÷ volume`, so `mass = density × volume` and
`volume = mass ÷ density`.

**Watch the units.** Density is usually given in **g/cm³** *or* **kg/m³**, and
1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.

*Worked example (real test question):* a 1.5 m cube of aluminum — find its mass.
- Volume = (1.5 m)³ = **3.375 m³**
- Aluminum density = 2.70 g/cm³ = **2700 kg/m³**
- Mass = 2700 kg/m³ × 3.375 m³ = **9112.5 kg** *(≈ 9100 kg)*

*(Note: some keys use "1.5 cubic meter" to mean a volume of 1.5 m³, not a
1.5 m cube. Read the wording. If volume = 1.5 m³: mass = 2700 × 1.5 = 4050 kg.)*

Density of **water = 1.00 g/cm³ = 1.00 g/mL** — memorize it; it's the reference
for every float/sink test and a stand-alone one-pointer.

### Oxides and why some metals resist acid at first

- **Aluminum** grows a thin, tough, transparent layer of **aluminum oxide
  (Al₂O₃)** the instant it meets air. This *passivation* layer protects the metal
  underneath — it's why aluminum doesn't visibly corrode and why it's slow to
  start reacting with acid.
- **Magnesium** forms a dull white coat of **magnesium oxide (MgO)** in air.
  Formation reaction: `2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO` — a **synthesis** (combination)
  reaction.
- **Iron** forms **hydrated iron(III) oxide (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O)** — rust — which is
  flaky and **not** protective, so iron keeps corroding.

### Reaction types (tested as multiple choice)

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthesis / combination** | A + B → AB | `2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO` |
| **Decomposition** | AB → A + B | `2 H₂O → 2 H₂ + O₂` |
| **Single replacement** | A + BC → AC + B | `Zn + 2 HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂` |
| **Double replacement** | AB + CD → AD + CB | `AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃` |
| **Combustion** | fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O | `CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O` |
| **Acid–base (neutralization)** | acid + base → salt + water | `HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O` |

A metal dissolving in HCl (`Mg + 2 HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂`) is a **single-replacement**
reaction — the metal displaces hydrogen.

### Alloys

An **alloy** is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements, made to
improve properties (strength, hardness, corrosion resistance).

| Alloy | Made of |
|---|---|
| Steel | iron + carbon (+ others) |
| Stainless steel | iron + chromium + nickel |
| Brass | copper + zinc |
| Bronze | copper + tin |
| Solder | tin + lead (or tin + silver) |

Common one-pointer: *"Steel is an alloy of which metal on the list?"* → **iron**.

## Chapter 7: Chemical Reactions and a First Look at Stoichiometry

> **Year 1 recap:** essentially none — year 1 avoided chemistry math. This chapter
> is entirely new, and the harder invitationals lean on it.

### Reading a chemical formula

- **Subscript** = number of atoms of the element right before it. `H₂O` = 2 H, 1 O.
- **Coefficient** = number of whole molecules. `3 H₂O` = 3 water molecules = 6 H, 3 O.
- **Parentheses**: `Ca(OH)₂` = 1 Ca, 2 O, 2 H. `Na₃C₆H₅O₇` = 3 Na, 6 C, 5 H, 7 O.
- **Dot hydrate**: `CaSO₄·2H₂O` = one CaSO₄ formula unit plus 2 waters loosely
  held in the crystal.

### Balancing equations

The same number of each atom must appear on both sides (mass is conserved).
Adjust **coefficients** only — never subscripts. Method: balance one element at a
time, save H and O for last, then double-check every element and re-count.

*Worked example — baking soda + citric acid (a real test question):*

`__ NaHCO₃ + __ H₃C₆H₅O₇ → __ CO₂ + __ H₂O + __ Na₃C₆H₅O₇`

Citrate (C₆H₅O₇) needs 3 Na, so 3 NaHCO₃ and 1 sodium citrate. That gives 3 C and
3 H from bicarbonate plus the citric acid's carbons/hydrogens. Balanced:

`3 NaHCO₃ + H₃C₆H₅O₇ → 3 CO₂ + 3 H₂O + Na₃C₆H₅O₇`

Check: Na 3=3, C 3+6=9 → 3+6=9 ✓, H 3+8=11 → 6+5=11 ✓, O 9+7=16 → 6+3+7=16 ✓.

### Moles, molar mass, and mass-to-mass

- A **mole** is 6.02 × 10²³ particles — a counting unit, like "dozen."
- **Molar mass** (g/mol) = sum of the atomic masses in the formula. Round atomic
  masses to 2 decimals from a periodic table: H 1.01, C 12.01, N 14.01, O 16.00,
  Na 22.99, S 32.07, Cl 35.45, Ca 40.08.
  - NaHCO₃ = 22.99 + 1.01 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = **84.01 g/mol**
  - Citric acid C₆H₈O₇ = 6(12.01) + 8(1.01) + 7(16.00) = **192.12 g/mol**
- **Moles = mass ÷ molar mass.**
- **Limiting reactant:** divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient; the
  smallest result runs out first and limits the product.
- **Product mass = (limiting moles ÷ its coefficient) × (product coefficient) ×
  (product molar mass).**

*Worked example:* 15 g NaHCO₃ + 10 g citric acid → how many grams of sodium
citrate (Na₃C₆H₅O₇, 258.07 g/mol)?
- 15 ÷ 84.01 = 0.1785 mol NaHCO₃ ; ÷ 3 = 0.0595
- 10 ÷ 192.12 = 0.0521 mol citric acid ; ÷ 1 = 0.0521 → **citric acid limits**
- citrate:citric acid is 1:1 → 0.0521 mol citrate
- 0.0521 × 258.07 = **13.4 g** sodium citrate

### Lewis dot structures (drawn on some tests)

A **Lewis structure** shows bonding pairs (lines) and lone pairs (dots) so every
atom reaches a full outer shell (8 electrons; 2 for H).

- **Water H₂O:** O in the center, single bonds to two H, two lone pairs on O, bent
  shape.
- **Carbon dioxide CO₂:** O=C=O, two double bonds, two lone pairs on each O,
  linear.
- **Baking soda NaHCO₃:** ionic — Na⁺ and the bicarbonate ion HCO₃⁻. In HCO₃⁻:
  central C double-bonded to one O, single-bonded to two more O; one of those
  single-bonded O carries the H, the other carries the negative charge.
- **Gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O:** ionic — Ca²⁺ and sulfate SO₄²⁻ (S center, 4 O around it,
  two S=O and two S–O⁻), plus two water molecules.

Point of the question is usually "ionic vs. covalent" and "does every atom have a
full octet," not perfect resonance drawing.

## Chapter 8: Hair

> **Year 1 recap:** cuticle / cortex / medulla; scale patterns (coronal, spinous,
> imbricate); human vs. dog vs. cat; the idea of a medullary index. What's new:
> the **growth cycle**, the **medullary-index calculation**, roots, and the
> forensic-timeline uses.

### Structure (recap, fast)

Three layers, **inner to outer: medulla → cortex → cuticle.**
- **Cuticle** — clear overlapping scales; protects the shaft; scale *pattern* is a
  species clue. Coronal (stacked crowns — small rodents, bats), spinous (petal-like,
  pointed — cat, seal), **imbricate** (flat, overlapping shingles — human, dog).
- **Cortex** — the bulk of the shaft; holds the **melanin** pigment granules that
  give hair its color; also has cortical fusi and (in animals) ovoid bodies.
- **Medulla** — the central core. Continuous, interrupted, fragmented, or absent.
  Pattern: **human** medulla is usually **fragmented or absent**; **animal**
  medulla is usually **continuous** and wide.

Hair is made of the protein **keratin**.

### The medullary index (now a calculation)

`Medullary Index (MI) = diameter of the medulla ÷ diameter of the whole hair
shaft`

Measured from a mounted hair under a microscope with a calibrated reticle, then
expressed as a fraction or decimal.

- **Human: MI < 1/3** (about 0.33) — the medulla is a thin thread or missing.
- **Animal: MI > 1/2** (about 0.5) — the medulla fills most of the shaft.
- Dog and cat both run high (≥ 1/2). Cat medulla often looks like a string of
  pearls / ladder; dog medulla is broad and often "stuffed."

*Example:* medulla 15 µm, shaft 60 µm → MI = 15/60 = **0.25 → human.**

### The hair growth cycle (four phases — asked with durations)

| Phase | What happens | Duration | Share of scalp hairs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anagen** | active growth; cells divide at the follicle | 2–7 years | ~85–90% |
| **Catagen** | transition; follicle shrinks, hair detaches from blood supply | ~2–3 weeks | ~1% |
| **Telogen** | resting; hair anchored but not growing | ~2–4 months | ~10–15% |
| **Exogen** | shedding; old hair falls out, new anagen hair pushes up | — (overlaps telogen) | — |

Consequences that get tested:
- A hair pulled during **anagen** brings a soft, elongated root sheath with
  follicular tissue and nuclear DNA — the best kind for DNA.
- A hair shed naturally in **telogen** has a hard, dry, **club-shaped root** and
  little or no nuclear DNA (mitochondrial DNA only).
- Human scalp hair grows about **1 cm per month** (~**0.5 in/month**, ~**6 in a
  year**). Growth is fastest in **summer/early autumn** and the *shedding* peak is
  in **autumn**.

### Roots

- **Human, naturally shed:** club-shaped, no tissue.
- **Human, forcibly pulled:** stretched sheath, follicular tag, sometimes
  bent/broken.
- **Dog:** spade- or brush-shaped root.
- **Cat:** frayed, "fibrillar" root.

### Forensic timeline uses

Because hair is **absorbent** and keratin **binds heavy metals**, hair records
the body's exposure history along its length as it grows. This makes hair the
sample of choice for detecting **arsenic** (and other heavy-metal) poisoning —
segmental analysis along the hair shows *when* the intake happened and whether it
was chronic. Common two-pointer: *"The absorbent property of hair makes it
important in what kind of poisoning, and why?"* → **arsenic / heavy-metal**
poisoning, because the metal binds to keratin and stays fixed in the hair,
preserving a datable timeline of exposure.

### The "hair keeps growing after death" myth

It does not. After death the skin **dehydrates and retracts**, pulling back from
the base of the hairs and the nails and making them *look* longer. No new growth
occurs.

### Rules constraint

Crime Busters hair ID is limited to **human, dog, and cat**. A microscope is the
right tool; the supervisor may provide one.

## Chapter 9: Fibers

> **Year 1 recap:** animal / vegetable / synthetic categories; direct vs.
> secondary transfer. What's new: the **burn-test flow chart** as a procedure,
> the **chemical origin** of synthetics, and naming specific fibers.

### The three classes

| Class | Made of | Examples | Under the microscope |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Animal (protein)** | keratin (hair) or fibroin (silk) | wool, cashmere, mohair, angora, alpaca; **silk** | scales (wool) like hair; silk is smooth, rod-like, triangular cross-section |
| **Vegetable (cellulose)** | cellulose | **cotton**, linen (flax), hemp, jute, ramie | cotton = flat twisted ribbon; bast fibers = bamboo-like nodes |
| **Synthetic / manufactured** | polymers | nylon, polyester, acrylic, olefin (polypropylene), spandex; **rayon & acetate = "regenerated" from cellulose (semi-synthetic)** | smooth, uniform, rod-like; may show extrusion lines and delustering pits (TiO₂) |

### The burn test (results may be provided; done as a demo only)

| Class | Approaching flame | In flame | Smell | Residue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Animal / protein** | curls away | burns slowly, sputters, self-extinguishes | **burning hair / feathers** | dark, brittle, crushable bead |
| **Vegetable / cellulose** | ignites readily | burns steadily, an afterglow | **burning paper / leaves** | light gray, feathery ash — no bead |
| **Synthetic** | **shrinks / melts away** from the flame | melts and drips, may burn | acrid, chemical, "sweet" plastic | hard, shiny, plastic bead |
| **Rayon** (regenerated cellulose) | ignites readily | burns *fast*, little melting | burning paper | little ash |

Flow chart form:

```
Does it smell like burning hair?
 ├─ Yes → ANIMAL (wool, silk)
 └─ No → Does it melt/shrink from the flame and leave a hard bead?
          ├─ Yes → SYNTHETIC
          └─ No → smells like burning paper, gray ash → VEGETABLE (cotton, linen)
```

### Where synthetics come from

Most synthetic fibers are polymers made from **petroleum** (and historically
**coal**). Nylon and polyester are polyamides/polyesters built from petrochemical
monomers. **Rayon** and **acetate** are the exceptions — they're made by
chemically **regenerating cellulose** from wood pulp, so they're called
*semi-synthetic* or *manufactured*.

### Moisture wicking (a real 3-point question)

*"What fiber is advertised to wick moisture, and how does it work?"* →
**Synthetic** (polyester, polypropylene). The fibers themselves don't absorb
water; instead the tiny channels **between** the fibers pull sweat outward by
**capillary action**, spreading it across the outer surface where it evaporates
quickly. Cotton, by contrast, absorbs water *into* the fiber and stays wet.

### Transfer (recap)

- **Direct (primary) transfer:** fiber goes straight from source to
  victim/suspect (the attacker's sweater onto the victim).
- **Secondary transfer:** fiber goes source → person A → person B (carpet fiber →
  victim → suspect who never touched the carpet). Secondary transfer is why a
  fiber match is **class** evidence, not proof of contact.

## Chapter 10: Paper Chromatography

> **Year 1 recap:** the team can run a strip and compute Rf. What's new: the
> vocabulary a key wants (stationary vs. mobile phase, what Rf physically means),
> the history question, and reading multi-spot chromatograms.

### The method

Separates a mixture (ink, food dye, plant pigment, juice) by how strongly each
component sticks to the paper vs. travels with the solvent.

- **Stationary phase:** the **paper** (cellulose, with water bound to it). It's
  polar.
- **Mobile phase:** the **solvent** that climbs the paper (water, salt water,
  alcohol, or a mix).
- A component that is **more soluble in the solvent** / **less attracted to the
  paper** travels **farther** (higher Rf). A component that grips the paper stays
  near the baseline (low Rf).

### Procedure (the version that scores)

1. Pencil (never pen) a **baseline** ~1.5 cm from the bottom.
2. Spot the sample on the baseline — small, concentrated, dried between
   additions. For liquids use a toothpick.
3. Stand the paper in solvent so the solvent is **below** the baseline — the spot
   must not be submerged.
4. Let the solvent climb to about **¾** of the paper. Mark the **solvent front**
   in pencil the instant you take it out — it evaporates.
5. Mark the center of each separated spot. Measure, from the baseline, to the
   solvent front and to each spot.

### Rf — retention (or retardation) factor

`Rf = distance traveled by the component ÷ distance traveled by the solvent front`

- Always between **0 and 1** (the component can't outrun the solvent that carries
  it). A component stuck on the baseline has **Rf = 0**; one that moves with the
  front has Rf ≈ 1.
- Rf is **dimensionless** and, under identical conditions (same paper, same
  solvent, same temperature), **reproducible** — that's what makes it useful for
  comparison.
- To match a crime-scene ink to a suspect's pen: run both under the same
  conditions and compare the **pattern of spots and their Rf values**. A match =
  same number of spots at the same Rf.

*Example:* yellow spot moved 3.0 cm, solvent moved 6.0 cm → Rf = 3.0/6.0 =
**0.50**. Orange spot moved 4.5 cm → Rf = 0.75. The orange dye is **more soluble
/ less retained**.

### History question

The word **chromatography** comes from Greek *chroma* ("color") + *graphein*
("to write"). Invented by the Russian botanist **Mikhail Tsvet** (Tswett) around
1900, separating plant pigments on a column of calcium carbonate.

## Chapter 11: Plastics and Polymers

> **Year 1 recap:** the seven rules plastics; density float test; burn behaviors.
> What's new: **thermoset vs. thermoplastic**, the **resin codes and their uses**,
> building a **density-column ladder**, polycarbonate, and the history.

### The rules plastics

PETE, HDPE, non-expanded PS, LDPE, PP, PVC, PMMA (and sometimes PC). Burn tests
are **not** conducted at competition, but burn *results may be given* to you.

| Code | Plastic | Density (g/cm³) | Water | Common uses | Burn behavior (if provided) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ♳ 1 | **PETE / PET** (polyethylene terephthalate) | 1.38–1.39 | **sinks** | soda bottles, polyester fiber, food jars | melts, sputters, black smoke, partly self-extinguishing, sweetish |
| ♴ 2 | **HDPE** (high-density polyethylene) | 0.94–0.97 | **floats** | milk jugs, detergent bottles, piping, cutting boards | blue flame/yellow tip, drips, **candle-wax** smell, keeps burning |
| ♵ 3 | **PVC / V** (polyvinyl chloride) | 1.16–1.45 (~1.38) | **sinks** | pipe, siding, wire insulation, "vinyl" | **self-extinguishes**, green-tinged flame, acrid (HCl) smell |
| ♶ 4 | **LDPE** (low-density polyethylene) | 0.92–0.94 | **floats** | grocery bags, squeeze bottles, film wrap | same as HDPE — candle-wax smell, drips |
| ♷ 5 | **PP** (polypropylene) | 0.90–0.92 | **floats** (highest) | yogurt tubs, bottle caps, straws, rope, living hinges | burns slower than PE, drips, sweeter/diesel smell |
| ♸ 6 | **PS** (polystyrene, non-expanded) | 1.04–1.07 | **sinks** | cutlery, CD cases, foam (expanded) | **dense black sooty smoke**, marigold/styrene smell |
| ♹ 7 | **PMMA** (acrylic, "plexiglass") | 1.17–1.20 | **sinks** | lenses, aquariums, signage, storm windows | bright crackling flame, fruity/floral smell |
| ♹ 7 | **PC** (polycarbonate) | 1.20–1.22 | **sinks** | bulletproof glass, water-cooler jugs, eyeglass lenses, DVDs | faint sweet/phenol smell, slow, sooty |

Memory hook for water: **only the polyolefins float** — PP, LDPE, HDPE (the
"poly-ethylene / -propylene" family). Everything else sinks.

### The density-column ladder

Water alone only splits floaters from sinkers. To separate *within* those groups,
use a series of liquids of known density and record float (F) / sink (S):

| Liquid | Density (g/cm³) |
|---|---|
| 70% isopropyl alcohol | ~0.79 |
| corn / vegetable oil | ~0.92 |
| water | 1.00 |
| 10% salt water | ~1.07 |
| saturated salt water | ~1.20 |
| glycerin | ~1.26 |
| light corn syrup | ~1.33 |
| maple syrup | ~1.33 |

Logic: a plastic **floats** in any liquid **denser** than it and **sinks** in any
liquid **less dense** than it. So the pair of liquids where it flips from float to
sink brackets its density. Example: floats in water (1.00), sinks in
70% isopropyl (0.79) → density between 0.79 and 1.00 → **PP, LDPE, or HDPE**;
then floats in corn oil (0.92) narrows it toward **PP** (0.90–0.92).

### Thermoplastic vs. thermoset (directly asked)

| | Thermoplastic | Thermoset |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | long chains, **not** cross-linked | chains **cross-linked** into one rigid network |
| Heat | **softens and re-melts** — can be remolded repeatedly | **does not melt** — it chars / decomposes |
| Curing | none | **cured** once (heat, UV, or a catalyst); irreversible |
| Recycling | mechanically recyclable | not meltable → not mechanically recyclable |
| Examples | **all the rules plastics** (PETE, HDPE, PVC, LDPE, PP, PS, PMMA, PC) | Bakelite, epoxy, polyurethane, melamine, vulcanized rubber |

Fill-in-the-blank you will see: *"All plastics in the rules occur in
thermo\_\_\_\_\_ form."* → **thermoplastic**.

### History and recycling

- The **first fully synthetic plastic** was **Bakelite**, made by **Leo
  Baekeland** in **1907** (US patent 1909). Earlier plastics (celluloid,
  Parkesine) were based on modified cellulose, so they don't count as "fully
  synthetic."
- Only about **9%** of all plastic ever made has been recycled (a common
  tiebreaker figure).
- **Resin codes 1–7** are identification codes, not a guarantee of
  recyclability — 1 and 2 are widely recycled, the rest much less so.

### LDPE vs. HDPE

Same chemistry (polyethylene), different chain packing. **HDPE** is more
crystalline → **stiffer, more opaque/cloudy, higher density**. **LDPE** is more
branched → **softer, more transparent/flexible, lower density**. Squeeze test:
crinkly and stiff = HDPE; soft and stretchy = LDPE.

## Chapter 12: DNA Evidence

> **Year 1 recap:** DNA structure, base pairing, replication, PCR, restriction
> enzymes, gel electrophoresis, STR/RFLP, electropherograms. Year 1 covered this
> well. What's new is mostly **reading a gel for a match** and a few acronyms.

### The essentials (recap)

- **Monomer / basic unit:** the **nucleotide** = phosphate + deoxyribose sugar +
  nitrogenous base.
- **Bases:** adenine, thymine, cytosine, guanine. **A–T, C–G.**
- Double helix; sugar-phosphate backbone; ~99.9% identical between any two humans
  — forensics targets the 0.1% that varies.
- **STRs (short tandem repeats):** short DNA sequences repeated a variable number
  of times at specific **loci**; the number of repeats varies between people.
  Modern profiling counts repeats at ~20 standard loci.
- **RNA** = ribonucleic acid (single-stranded; ribose instead of deoxyribose;
  uracil replaces thymine).
- **Chromosomes:** humans have **46** (23 pairs) — one of each pair from each
  parent. In eukaryotic cells DNA is packaged into chromosomes in the nucleus;
  in bacteria it floats free.

### PCR — polymerase chain reaction (asked as "the 3 stages")

PCR makes millions of copies of a DNA segment so there's enough to see on a gel.
Each cycle has **three stages**, repeated ~30 times:

1. **Denaturation** (~95 °C) — heat splits the double helix into two single
   strands.
2. **Annealing** (~55 °C) — short **primers** bind to the ends of the target
   region.
3. **Extension / synthesis** (~72 °C) — **DNA polymerase** (heat-stable **Taq
   polymerase**) adds nucleotides, building a new complementary strand.

**PCR happens *before* gel electrophoresis** — you have to amplify the tiny
sample into a visible amount first. (Invented by Kary Mullis; Nobel Prize 1993.)

### Sources of DNA at a scene

Blood, semen, saliva, skin / epithelial "touch" cells, **hair with the follicle
attached**, bone, teeth, nasal mucus, dandruff, urine and feces (few cells).
Hair *shafts* without a root give only mitochondrial DNA.

### Reading a gel (the Crime Busters task)

Gel electrophoresis sorts DNA fragments by size. DNA is **negatively charged**,
so it migrates toward the **positive** electrode. **Smaller fragments travel
farther**; larger fragments stay near the wells. The result is a ladder of bands
per lane.

To find a match: line up the **crime-scene (CS) lane** against each **suspect
lane** and check whether the bands sit at the **same heights**. The suspect whose
band pattern lines up with the crime-scene pattern is the source. A "ladder" or
"marker" lane of known sizes may be included for scale.

### Acronyms (short answer)

| Acronym | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **CODIS** | **Combined DNA Index System** (FBI) — *not* "Combined Official DNA Identification System" |
| **AFIS** | Automated Fingerprint Identification System |
| **IAFIS** | Integrated AFIS (FBI, 1999) — replaced in 2014 by **NGI** (Next Generation Identification) |
| **NCIC** | National Crime Information Center |
| **PCR** | polymerase chain reaction (DNA amplification; Kary Mullis, Nobel 1993) |
| **RFLP** | restriction fragment length polymorphism (older method) |

## Chapter 13: Blood: Typing and Chemistry

> **Year 1 recap:** none — `bloodtype.pptx` was empty. This chapter is entirely
> new, and blood appears on essentially every recent invitational.

### ABO and Rh types

Red blood cells carry **antigens** on their surface — A, B, both, or neither —
and the plasma carries **antibodies** against whatever antigen the person lacks.

| Type | Antigens on cells | Antibodies in plasma | Can donate red cells to | Can receive red cells from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | A | anti-B | A, AB | A, O |
| **B** | B | anti-A | B, AB | B, O |
| **AB** | A and B | none | AB only | everyone — **universal recipient** |
| **O** | none | anti-A and anti-B | everyone — **universal donor** | O only |

**Rh factor (D antigen):** Rh-positive cells have the D antigen; Rh-negative
don't. So the full type is e.g. **A+** (A antigen, D antigen) or **O−** (neither).
- **Universal red-cell donor: O−.** **Universal recipient: AB+.**

**US frequency, most common to rarest** (memorize this order):
**O+ > A+ > B+ > O− > A− > AB+ > B− > AB−.**

### Reading an agglutination (blood-typing) grid

Drops of the blood are mixed with three test sera: **anti-A, anti-B, anti-D**.
**Agglutination (clumping)** means that antigen is present.

| Anti-A | Anti-B | Anti-D | → Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| clump | — | clump | **A+** |
| clump | — | — | **A−** |
| — | clump | clump | **B+** |
| — | — | clump | **O+** |
| clump | clump | clump | **AB+** |
| — | — | — | **O−** |

So: clump with anti-A → has A; clump with anti-B → has B; clump with anti-D →
Rh-positive. Both anti-A and anti-B → AB. Neither → O.

### Blood-type genetics (Punnett questions)

Alleles: **I^A** and **I^B** are **codominant**; **i** is recessive. Genotypes:
AA or AO → type A; BB or BO → type B; AB → type AB; OO → type O.

*Common question:* a **type A** parent × a **type B** parent — what can the child
**not** be? → They *can* have an A, B, AB, or O child (if each parent is a
carrier of O), so with the choices "A / B / O / none of the above," the answer is
**none of the above** — every type is possible.

*Also:* two type-O parents can only have type-O children. A type-AB parent can
never have a type-O child.

### Blood chemistry

- **Hemoglobin** is the iron-containing protein in red cells that carries O₂.
- **Oxygenated** blood (arteries, fresh exposure to air) is **bright red**;
  **deoxygenated** blood (veins) is **dark red**.
- **When blood is exposed to air/oxygen:** O₂ binds hemoglobin (brightens the
  color); then over minutes the platelets activate and it **clots**; over hours
  the hemoglobin **oxidizes** to methemoglobin and then to brown **hemichrome**,
  so a dried stain turns **reddish-brown → brown**. The stain also loses CO₂,
  nudging pH up. Age of a bloodstain can be roughly estimated from this color
  progression.
- Blood pH in the body is tightly held at **~7.35–7.45** (slightly basic).

### Presumptive blood tests (short answer — reagents not supplied)

| Test | Reaction | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Kastle-Meyer** | phenolphthalein + H₂O₂ → **bright pink** if blood present | hemoglobin acts like peroxidase; presumptive only |
| **Luminol** | sprayed; **glows blue** in the dark on contact with blood | finds cleaned-up / trace blood; can react with some metals and bleach |
| **Hemastix / leucomalachite green** | color change | field presumptive |
| **Precipitin test** | anti-human serum → confirms blood is **human** | species determination |

Superglue/**cyanoacrylate** fuming and **Ardrox** (fluoresces yellow under UV)
are **fingerprint** developers, not blood tests — a favorite trick question.

## Chapter 14: Blood Spatter

> **Year 1 recap:** "velocity," "angle," drop height vs. diameter — but the rules
> then said *no calculations*. Recent invitationals **do** ask for the angle
> calculation, so that's the year-2 addition.

### What a spatter pattern can tell you (asked as a "list 6–9 things")

- the **direction** each drop was traveling (from the tail / satellites)
- the **angle of impact** (from the stain's width-to-length ratio)
- the **velocity / force** category — low, medium, or high
- the likely **cause** — dripping, blunt force, stabbing, gunshot, cast-off,
  arterial
- the **area of convergence** and **area of origin** — where the blood came from,
  including height
- the **position and movement** of the victim and attacker
- the **number of blows** (from counting cast-off arcs)
- whether a person or object was present (a **void**), and whether the scene was
  **altered / cleaned**
- with lab work: the **blood type** and a **DNA profile**
- a rough **sequence / timeline** of events

### Drop shape, size, and direction

- A drop falling **straight down** onto a level surface makes a **round** stain.
- A drop striking at an **angle** makes an **elongated/elliptical** stain with a
  pointed **tail**. **The tail points in the direction of travel** (toward where
  the blood was going).
- **Satellite spatter** — small droplets thrown off around a larger parent drop.
  **Spines** — the pointed edge projections. Both point outward in the direction
  of motion.
- **Drop diameter vs. fall height:** a drop released from higher up hits with
  more energy and spreads into a **larger-diameter** stain — the diameter
  **increases with height** up to a point (roughly 7 ft / ~20 ft/s), after which
  the drop reaches terminal velocity and the size stops growing. Higher falls
  also produce more **scalloped edges and satellite spatter**.
- **Passive drops** are typically **4 mm or larger**; a single free-falling blood
  drop is about 0.05 mL.

### Angle of impact — the calculation

`sin(impact angle) = width of the stain ÷ length of the stain`

so

`impact angle = arcsin(width ÷ length)` &nbsp; (calculator: `sin⁻¹`)

- A **round** stain (width ≈ length) → arcsin(1) = **90°** (drop fell straight
  down / hit perpendicular).
- A long thin stain → small ratio → small angle (a glancing hit, ~15–30°).

*Example:* stain 6 mm wide, 12 mm long → sin θ = 6/12 = 0.5 → θ = **30°**.

### Area of convergence and area of origin

- **Area of convergence:** draw a straight line back through the long axis of
  several stains; where the lines cross (in 2-D, on the wall/floor) is the
  convergence — directly below/beside the source.
- **Area of origin:** the 3-D point in space where the blood came from — found by
  also using the impact angles (the "stringing" method) to get height.

### Velocity categories (mechanism, not just speed)

| Category | Drop size | Typical cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Low velocity** (passive) | large, > 4 mm — drips, pools, flows | gravity only; dripping from a wound; ~5 ft/s or less |
| **Medium velocity** | ~1–4 mm | **blunt-force** trauma, stabbing, some cast-off; ~5–25 ft/s |
| **High velocity** | fine **mist**, < 1 mm | **gunshot**, high-speed machinery, explosion; > 100 ft/s |

Also: **cast-off** (flung off a swinging weapon — a line of stains), **arterial
spurt** (large volume, rhythmic, bright red), **transfer/contact** (an object or
hand pressed into wet blood — sometimes a recognizable pattern), **void** (a gap
where something blocked the spatter — tells you a person/object was there).

## Chapter 15: Glass Fracture

> **Year 1 recap:** none. Glass shows up on recent invitationals as a
> short-answer / multiple-choice topic.

### The two crack families

- **Radial cracks** radiate **out** from the impact point like spokes. They form
  **first**, and they start on the side **opposite** the force (the surface that
  was in tension).
- **Concentric cracks** form circles **around** the impact point, connecting the
  radials. They form **second**, on the **same** side as the force.

### The 3R rule

**Radial cracks — stress lines meet the surface at a Right angle on the Reverse
(rear) side** — i.e., the side the force came *from*. (For concentric cracks it's
the opposite side.) Reading the tiny curved "rib marks" (conchoidal / hackle
lines) on a crack edge tells you which side was struck.

### Cone (Hertzian) fracture

A fast projectile (BB, bullet) punches a **cone-shaped hole** — narrow on the
entry side, wider on the exit side. So the **wider opening faces the direction the
projectile was traveling** — that gives you direction of fire.

### Sequencing multiple impacts

A crack **stops** when it runs into a pre-existing crack. So if fracture lines
from hole B terminate at lines from hole A, then **A happened first**.

### Speed vs. cracking

A **high-velocity** projectile makes a clean hole with **little** radial
cracking (the glass doesn't have time to flex). A **low-velocity** impact
(a thrown rock, a fist) produces **extensive** radial and concentric cracking.
Heat and slow pressure produce wavy, non-radial cracks.

## Chapter 16: Soil

> **Year 1 recap:** sand / silt / clay / loam and their drainage behavior. What's
> new: the **soil texture triangle** (on every recent test), **particle-size
> cutoffs**, and what **color** and **pH** mean.

### Particle sizes (USDA scale)

| Separate | Diameter |
|---|---|
| Sand | 2.0 – 0.05 mm (gritty, visible) |
| Silt | 0.05 – 0.002 mm (floury, smooth) |
| Clay | **< 0.002 mm** (sticky when wet, hard when dry) |

Anything larger than 2 mm is **gravel** and isn't counted in texture.

### The soil texture triangle

Given the **percent sand, silt, and clay** (they sum to 100), the triangle gives
a **texture class name**. How to read it:
- Find the **% clay** on the left side, follow its line **horizontally**.
- Find the **% sand** on the bottom, follow its line **up-left** (parallel to the
  right side).
- Find the **% silt** on the right side, follow its line **down-left** (parallel
  to the left side).
- The zone where they meet is the class.

Anchor points to memorize:
- **Loam** ≈ 40% sand / 40% silt / 20% clay — the balanced middle.
- **Sandy loam** — sand-dominated (~60–70% sand), low clay.
- **Clay** — > 40% clay.
- **Silt loam** — silt-dominated (> 50% silt), low clay.
- **Clay loam** ≈ 30–35% clay, roughly even sand and silt.

*Example:* 60% sand, 30% silt, 10% clay → **sandy loam.**
*Example:* 35% clay, 40% silt, 25% sand → **clay loam.**

### Color (what it tells you)

| Color | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Red / yellow / orange** | **iron oxides** — well-drained, oxidized soil |
| **Dark brown / black** | **organic matter** (humus) — topsoil, high fertility |
| **Gray / blue-gray (gleyed)** | waterlogged, oxygen-poor — iron is chemically reduced |
| **White / pale / cream** | **carbonates, salts, or silica** — leached soil, or lime/sand/gypsum content |

### Other forensic axes

- **pH** — soils range roughly 4 (acidic, pine forest) to 9 (alkaline, arid /
  limey). A garden amended with lime or ammonia will read differently from
  surrounding soil.
- **Density-gradient column, mineral content under a lens, and organic debris**
  (pollen, seeds, fibers) all add comparison points.
- Soil is **class evidence** — a comparison can strongly link a suspect's shoes
  to a scene or **exclude** them, but rarely identifies one location uniquely.
- *"How is soil texture relevant to forensics?"* → soil clinging to a shoe, tire,
  or shovel can be compared (texture, color, pH, minerals) to soil at the scene
  to place a suspect there or rule them out.

## Chapter 17: Impressions — Fingerprints, Shoeprints, and Tire Tracks

> **Year 1 recap:** loop / whorl / arch; ulnar vs. radial; delta counts;
> minutiae types; basic shoe/tire direction and speed. What's new: the **full
> IAFIS pattern set**, **print types and how they form**, developing latents, the
> vocabulary (dactyloscopy, dermatoglyphics), and **class vs. individual** for
> footwear.

### The three pattern families (recap) and their frequencies

| Pattern | Deltas | Approx. frequency |
|---|---|---|
| **Loop** | 1 | **60–65%** |
| **Whorl** | 2 | **30–35%** |
| **Arch** | 0 | **~5%** |

**Loops** open toward one side: **ulnar loop** opens toward the little finger
(most common); **radial loop** opens toward the thumb.

### The full IAFIS / Henry classification (eight patterns)

| Family | Sub-types |
|---|---|
| Arch | **plain arch**, **tented arch** |
| Loop | **radial loop**, **ulnar loop** |
| Whorl | **plain whorl**, **central pocket loop whorl**, **double loop whorl**, **accidental whorl** |

- **Plain whorl** — one or more complete circuits, two deltas, an imaginary line
  between the deltas crosses a recurve.
- **Central pocket loop whorl** — a loop with a small whorl/pocket at the core;
  that line does *not* cross a recurve.
- **Double loop whorl** — two separate loop formations (an "S" / yin-yang).
- **Accidental whorl** — a combination of two different pattern types, or one that
  fits no other category.

### Minutiae (Galton details)

Ridge ending, **bifurcation** (a ridge splits in two), dot, island / short ridge,
lake / enclosure, spur / hook, bridge, crossover, **delta** (a triangle where
ridge flows meet), **core** (center of a loop/whorl). Identification is based on
the **type, position, and orientation** of a set of matching minutiae. There is
no legally fixed minimum count in the US, but examiners typically look for ~8–12
concordant points.

### Print types at a scene (how each forms)

| Type | How it's made | How it's found |
|---|---|---|
| **Latent** | invisible — sweat, oils, and amino acids left by the friction ridges | must be **developed**: dusting powder, superglue (cyanoacrylate) fuming, ninhydrin (amino acids → purple), iodine fuming, silver nitrate, fluorescent dyes + ALS |
| **Patent** | visible — the finger was coated in a substance (**blood, ink, paint, grease, dust**) and transferred a print | photographed directly |
| **Plastic / impressed** | a 3-D mold — the finger pressed into a soft material (**putty, wax, soft clay, tacky paint, chocolate**) | photographed with oblique lighting; cast |

### Vocabulary (one-pointers)

- **Dermatoglyphics** — the study of friction-ridge skin patterns.
- **Dactyloscopy** — fingerprint identification / comparison.
- Friction ridges form in the **dermal papillae** — the **papillary layer of the
  dermis** — around fetal weeks 10–16, and are **permanent and unique** (even
  identical twins differ).
- **Adermatoglyphia** — a rare inherited condition: born with **no fingerprints**
  ("immigration-delay disease").
- **Hexadactyly / polydactyly** — born with a sixth finger or toe.
- Handwriting analysis is **graphology** (informal) / **questioned-document
  examination** (forensic).

### Footwear and tire impressions

- **2-D** (dust, residue, wet transfer on a hard floor) vs. **3-D** (pressed into
  soil, snow, sand — can be cast with dental stone).
- **Class characteristics:** size, overall tread design, brand/model, wear
  pattern general shape.
- **Individual characteristics:** a specific nick, cut, gouge, wear spot, or
  embedded pebble — these can tie a print to **one** shoe.
- **Gait / travel:** direction from the toe-push and the shape of the print;
  **stride length** (heel of one foot to heel of the *same* foot again) and
  **step length** (heel to heel of *opposite* feet); a longer stride and deeper
  toe impression suggest running. Tires: arrowhead tread points in the direction
  of travel on drive tires; long skid marks mean higher speed before stopping.
  Per the rules, **no calculations are expected** for tracks or spatter direction
  — but the spatter *angle* calculation in Chapter 14 is now fair game.

## Chapter 18: Writing the Analysis

The analysis section is worth about **25%** of the whole test and rewards a
**structure**, not just the right name. Real keys award roughly:

- a large block of points for **naming the correct culprit(s)** (there may be more
  than one, or an accomplice),
- **1 point per piece of evidence** correctly tied to a suspect **with a stated
  reason**,
- points for a **plausible motive** for each major suspect,
- points for explaining **why each non-culprit is cleared**.

Partial credit is real: naming the right culprit with no supporting evidence
often caps at a fraction of the available points.

### The template the team should write to

> **Culprit:** _______ (and accomplice, if any)
>
> **Motive:** one or two sentences — why this person.
>
> **Evidence against the culprit** — a bullet per item:
> - *Powder 3 = gypsum* → Suspect A does drywall work.
> - *Fiber 1 = wool (animal)* → Suspect A wore a wool coat that night.
> - *Soil = sandy loam* → matches the lot behind Suspect A's shop.
> - *Fingerprint (double loop whorl)* → matches Suspect A's right index.
>
> **Timeline:** how the physical evidence fits the sequence of events / alibis.
>
> **Why the others are cleared** — a short paragraph per remaining suspect:
> - *Suspect B* — the vinegar and vitamin C match, but those are things half the
>   suspects use; no unique physical evidence; alibi holds.
> - *Suspect C* — strong **motive** (the lawsuit) but **zero** matching physical
>   evidence; motive alone isn't proof.

### Coaching points

- **Every** suspect gets addressed — guilty or not. Skipping the innocent ones
  leaves points on the table.
- Distinguish **"matches but is common"** (weak) from **"matches and is unusual"**
  (strong). Gypsum and wool are more incriminating than salt and water.
- Call out **motive without evidence** and **evidence without motive** explicitly
  — graders look for that judgment.
- Watch for **planted / secondary-transfer** evidence and for a **void** or a
  missing item that points somewhere.
- Budget time: on a 50-minute test, leave **at least 10 minutes** for the
  analysis write-up. It's the highest points-per-minute once the IDs are done.

## Appendix D: Flow Charts and Dichotomous Keys

### White-powder key (6-powder core set)

```
1. Strong fizz with acid?  ── yes ──► 2      ── no ──► 3
2. Fully dissolves in water? ── yes ──► BAKING SODA (NaHCO3)
                             ── no  ──► CHALK / calcium carbonate (CaCO3)
3. Blue-black with iodine?   ── yes ──► CORNSTARCH ((C6H10O5)n)
                             ── no  ──► 4
4. Fully dissolves in water? ── no  ──► GYPSUM (CaSO4·2H2O)
                             ── yes ──► 5
5. Cubic crystals, no melt on heat? ── yes ──► TABLE SALT (NaCl)
                                    ── no  ──► SUGAR (C12H22O11)
```

### Extended powder key (adds the acids and fizz-in-water group)

```
1. Fizzes in PLAIN WATER (no acid added)? ── yes ──► ALKA-SELTZER or BAKING POWDER
                                                       (iodine + → baking powder;
                                                        iodine – → Alka-Seltzer)
                                            ── no ──► 2
2. Water solution is clearly ACIDIC (pH < 5)? ── yes ──► 3   ── no ──► 4
3. Turns iodine COLORLESS?  ── yes ──► VITAMIN C (ascorbic acid)
                            ── no  ──► CITRIC ACID (very soluble) or
                                       CREAM OF TARTAR (only slightly soluble)
4. Strong fizz when ACID added? ── yes ──► carbonate group (baking soda / chalk /
                                            washing soda — split by solubility & pH)
                                ── no ──► starch/iodine test, then solubility, per key above
```

### Liquid key

```
1. Strong pungent smell?  ── yes ──► AMMONIA (basic, pH ~11)  [or bleach — chlorine smell]
                          ── no ──► 2
2. Sharp sour smell, pH 2–3? ── yes ──► VINEGAR or LEMON JUICE (lemon is cloudy/yellow)
                             ── no ──► 3
3. Bubbles/foams on a drop of blood or potato? ── yes ──► HYDROGEN PEROXIDE
                                               ── no ──► 4
4. Evaporates fast, medicinal smell, flammable? ── yes ──► ISOPROPYL ALCOHOL
                                                ── no ──► WATER (pH 7, no smell, no residue)
                                                          / SALT or SUGAR water (residue on drying)
```

### Fiber burn-test flow chart

```
Smells like burning HAIR/feathers, self-extinguishes, crushable bead?
  ── yes ──► ANIMAL / protein (wool, silk)
  ── no ──► Melts & shrinks AWAY from flame, hard shiny bead, chemical smell?
             ── yes ──► SYNTHETIC (nylon, polyester, acrylic, olefin)
             ── no ──► Burns steadily, smells like PAPER, soft gray ash, no bead?
                        ── yes ──► VEGETABLE / cellulose (cotton, linen)
                        (burns very fast, little ash → RAYON, regenerated cellulose)
```

### Plastic identification (density + provided burn result)

```
1. Floats in water?
   ── yes ──► PP, LDPE, or HDPE   → float in corn oil (0.92)? yes → PP
                                    → else squeeze: stiff/cloudy → HDPE; soft/clear → LDPE
   ── no ──► 2
2. Burn result = dense BLACK sooty smoke, styrene smell? ── yes ──► PS
3. Burn result = self-extinguishes, acrid/HCl smell?     ── yes ──► PVC
4. Sinks, bright crackling flame, fruity smell?          ── yes ──► PMMA (acrylic)
5. Sinks, melts with black smoke, partly self-extinguishing, from a bottle? ──► PETE
6. Sinks, very hard/clear, faint sweet smell, "bulletproof"? ──► PC
```

## Appendix E: Glossary

**Agglutination** — clumping of red blood cells when antibodies meet their
matching antigen; how blood typing is read.
**Amylose / amylopectin** — the linear / branched glucose polymers in starch;
amylose gives the blue-black iodine color.
**Anagen / catagen / telogen / exogen** — the growth, transition, resting, and
shedding phases of the hair cycle.
**Area of convergence / origin** — the 2-D point the spatter came from / the 3-D
point in space including height.
**Bakelite** — the first fully synthetic plastic (Baekeland, 1907); a thermoset.
**Cast-off** — spatter flung from a moving bloody object, forming a line of
stains.
**Chain of custody** — the documented handling history of evidence from scene to
court.
**Charge-transfer complex** — the light-absorbing partnership between iodine and
the amylose helix.
**Class vs. individual characteristics** — features that place an item in a group
vs. features unique to one source.
**Clathrate / inclusion complex** — one molecule physically caged in another
(iodine inside amylose).
**CODIS** — Combined DNA Index System (FBI).
**Cone (Hertzian) fracture** — the cone-shaped hole a projectile makes in glass;
wider end faces travel direction.
**Concentric / radial cracks** — circular / spoke-like glass cracks; radials form
first.
**Cortex / cuticle / medulla** — the middle / outer / core layers of a hair.
**Dactyloscopy** — fingerprint identification.
**Delta** — a triangular ridge formation; loops have 1, whorls 2, arches 0.
**Dermatoglyphics** — the study of friction-ridge skin.
**Dichotomous key** — a branching either/or key that leads to an identification.
**Locard's Exchange Principle** — every contact leaves a trace.
**Latent / patent / plastic print** — invisible / visible-by-transfer / 3-D
impressed fingerprint.
**Medullary index** — medulla width ÷ shaft width; human < 1/3, animal > 1/2.
**Minutiae (Galton details)** — small ridge features (endings, bifurcations,
dots…) used to match prints.
**Mobile / stationary phase** — the moving solvent / the fixed paper in
chromatography.
**Mole** — 6.02 × 10²³ particles.
**Passivation** — a thin oxide layer (Al₂O₃, MgO) that protects the metal beneath.
**pH** — "potential of hydrogen"; −log₁₀[H⁺]; < 7 acidic, > 7 basic.
**Presumptive vs. confirmatory test** — fast/sensitive/not-specific vs.
identity-certain.
**Reducing sugar** — a sugar that reduces Cu²⁺ in Benedict's solution (glucose,
fructose, maltose, lactose); sucrose and starch are non-reducing.
**Retention factor (Rf)** — distance a component travels ÷ distance the solvent
travels; 0–1.
**Rh factor (D antigen)** — the +/− part of a blood type.
**STR (short tandem repeat)** — a variable-repeat DNA region used for profiling.
**Soil texture triangle** — chart converting % sand/silt/clay into a texture
class.
**Synthesis / decomposition / single & double replacement** — the four basic
reaction types.
**Thermoplastic / thermoset** — re-meltable / permanently cured plastic.
**Universal donor / recipient** — O− / AB+.
**Void** — a gap in a spatter pattern where something blocked the blood.
**3R rule** — Radial cracks meet the glass surface at a Right angle on the
Reverse (force) side.

## Appendix F: Equipment and What to Bring

The supervisor provides iodine reagent, 1 M HCl, chromatography supplies, a waste
container, and a distilled-water wash bottle (and sometimes a microscope or
candle). Bring the rest:

- **Safety:** goggles (each person), apron/lab coat, gloves; hair ties.
- **Test tools:** spot plates or a white tray, test tubes + rack, droppers /
  pipettes, stirring rods, tweezers/forceps, spatula/scoopula, toothpicks.
- **Measuring:** a non-programmable calculator **with `sin⁻¹`**, a metric ruler, a
  small digital scale if allowed, a 10 mL graduated cylinder.
- **Reference:** magnet, pH paper (full range), hand lens (10×), a small
  microscope or loupe if you have one.
- **Density set:** small bottles of isopropyl alcohol, vegetable oil, saturated
  salt water, corn syrup (labeled with densities).
- **Chromatography:** filter paper / coffee filters, pencil, a covered cup or
  beaker.
- **Consumables / cleanup:** paper towels, a squeeze bottle of water, a small
  trash bag, distilled water.
- **The note sheets:** one 8.5 × 11 sheet per person, both sides, in a sheet
  protector.

Build a personal **equipment checklist** and pack from it every time — a missing
tool is a silent points loss.
