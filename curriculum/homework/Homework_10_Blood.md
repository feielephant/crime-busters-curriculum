## Part B: Facts (1 pt each)

## Part C: Genetics (2 pts each)

1. Two type-O parents — what blood types can their children be?
2. A type-AB parent and a type-O parent — what types can the child be? What can

## Part D: Chemistry (2 pts each)

## Part E: Scenario (4 pts)

At a scene, there is the victim's blood (type O+) and a **second** blood sample
by the back door that types as **A−**. The four suspects type: O+, A−, B+, AB+.
1. Which suspect does the second sample implicate?
2. Why is the *existence* of a second, non-victim blood sample an important clue?
3. Does the blood type prove that suspect committed the crime? Explain.

## Answer Key

**Part A:** 1. AB+.  2. B+.  3. A−.  4. O+.  5. O−.

**Part B:**
1. Donor O−; recipient AB+.
2. O+, A+, B+, O−, A−, AB+, B−, AB−.
3. B antigen on the cells; anti-A antibody in the plasma.

**Part C:**
1. Only type O.
2. Child can be A or B; **cannot** be O and cannot be AB.
3. A, B, AB, or O (all four).

**Part D:**
1. Oxygen binds the hemoglobin (turns brighter red); platelets activate and it
   clots; the hemoglobin then oxidizes and the dried stain turns reddish-brown
   to brown over hours.
2. Hemoglobin.
3. Kastle-Meyer → bright pink; luminol → glows blue in the dark. (Also Hemastix.)
4. No — it develops latent **fingerprints**.

**Part E:**
1. The **A−** suspect.
2. It suggests the attacker was also injured (e.g., cut on the victim's broken
   glass or their own weapon) — placing an injured person at the scene.
3. No — blood type is class evidence. A− is fairly uncommon but still shared by
   millions of people; it narrows the field but doesn't identify one person. DNA
   from the sample would.
