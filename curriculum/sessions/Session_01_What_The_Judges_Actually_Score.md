# Session 1: What the Judges Actually Score
### Crime Busters Coach Script — [~90 min]

## Before You Start

- **Learning goals:** By the end, each kid can (1) state Locard's Exchange
  Principle in their own words, (2) tell class evidence from individual evidence
  and give an example of each, (3) explain why "circumstantial" is not the same as
  "weak," (4) lay out the five scored sections of the test and roughly what each
  is worth, and (5) write a short analysis paragraph that follows the
  culprit → evidence-with-reasons → motive → clear-the-others structure.
- **This is a year-2 season opener.** The team has done Crime Busters before, so
  this session is *not* a re-introduction to forensics. It's about how the event
  is actually scored and how to think like a grader — plus handing out the home
  practice kits.
- **Materials:**
  - The Knowledge Document, Chapters 1, 2, and 18 (read them before this session).
  - Printed **home practice kits** — one per kid (see
    `../home_labs/Kit_Assembly_Guide.md`) — plus a copy of the **Home Lab Safety
    Agreement** for each family.
  - Printed **Mini-Case Packet** (below) — one per kid.
  - Printed **Evidence Sorting Grid** and **Analysis Template** (below).
  - Whiteboard + markers; pens/pencils.
  - Optional: a copy of one real past invitational test (from your `raw/` folder
    or a test-exchange site) just to show them the physical format — don't work it
    today.
- **Prep:**
  - Assemble the home kits. This takes about an hour the first time — do it a few
    days ahead.
  - Write the five score categories on the whiteboard before kids arrive (leave
    the percentages blank — they'll guess).
  - Print everything below.
- **Vocabulary this session:** Locard's Exchange Principle · class characteristic
  · individual characteristic · direct evidence · circumstantial evidence ·
  presumptive test · confirmatory test · chain of custody.

> **If you only have 60 minutes:** Skip the "Guess the Percentages" warm-up
> (0:10–0:18) and go straight from the hook into the evidence-types teaching. In
> the Mini-Case activity, do only Part A (sorting) and assign Part B (the writeup)
> as the first homework. Keep the kit handout and the safety talk — those are
> non-negotiable.

## Segment-by-Segment Script

### 0:00–0:10 (10 min) — Hook: "You Already Know the Science. This Year We Learn the Test."

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Last year you all learned a ton of forensics — fingerprints, hair, plastics,
  chromatography, all of it. You're not beginners anymore. So this year we're
  doing two things differently.

  First: we're going deeper. Last year a question might ask 'what powder is
  this?' This year the same question also wants the chemical formula, and a
  follow-up asks *why* the test works. We'll build all that up.

  Second — and this is today — we're going to get really good at the *test
  itself*. Because here's a thing that surprises people: two teams can have the
  exact same science knowledge and score thirty points apart, just because one
  team knows how the graders hand out points and the other doesn't.

  So today isn't a science lesson. It's a 'how does this test actually work'
  lesson. And at the end I'm giving each of you a kit to take home so you can
  practice the hands-on stuff between our meetings."

  **[ASK THE TEAM: Last year, when you finished a Crime Busters test, what part
  felt like it went the worst — the powders, the physical evidence, the writing
  at the end? Take a guess each.]**
  - Whatever they say, note it — you'll come back to it all season. Most teams say
    the powders (slow) or the final analysis (rushed). Both are exactly what this
    curriculum spends the most time on.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** Ask: "What are the two things we're doing differently this
  year?" (Going deeper into the science; getting good at how the test is scored.)

---

### 0:10–0:18 (8 min) — Warm-Up: Guess the Percentages

- **Goal:** kids internalize that qualitative analysis + analysis writeup are
  three-quarters of the score, so that's where the practice time goes.

- **[ACTIVITY: Guess the Percentages]**
  On the whiteboard you have the five categories. Have the team guess what
  percent of the score each is worth (they should total 100). Let them argue it
  out, then fill in the real numbers:

  | Category | Worth |
  |---|---|
  | Qualitative analysis (powders, liquids, metals) | **~50%** |
  | Analysis writeup (name the culprit, justify it) | **~25%** |
  | Crime-scene physical evidence (prints, DNA, soil, spatter) | ~10% |
  | Polymers (hair, fiber, plastic) | ~10% |
  | Paper chromatography | ~5% |

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Look at the top two. Identifying powders and liquids and metals, plus writing
  the analysis — that's **seventy-five percent** of the whole test. The cool
  stuff, fingerprints and DNA and blood spatter, all of that together is about a
  quarter. We'll learn all of it, but you can see where the practice time needs
  to go."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "If you had 50 minutes for a test and had to protect time for
  one section, which is it?" (Qualitative analysis — it's half the points and
  it's slow. Second answer: leave time for the analysis writeup.)

---

### 0:18–0:40 (22 min) — Direct Teaching: How a Grader Thinks

- **Goal:** kids can define and give examples of the evidence-type pairs and
  Locard's principle, because these are recurring short-answer questions *and*
  the vocabulary they'll use in every analysis writeup.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Four ideas. Every one of them shows up as a short-answer question, and every
  one of them changes how you write your analysis. I'll teach them, then we'll
  use them on a real mini-case.

  **One: Locard's Exchange Principle.** Say it with me: *every contact leaves a
  trace.* A French scientist named Edmond Locard, over a hundred years ago. When
  two things touch, stuff moves *both* directions. The criminal leaves hair and
  fibers and prints and mud at the scene — and they carry away carpet fibers,
  broken glass, the victim's blood, pollen. This is the *whole reason* trace
  evidence works. If contact didn't leave a trace, we'd have no job.

  **Two: class evidence versus individual evidence.** Class evidence puts
  something in a *group*. 'A men's size-10 running shoe.' 'Cotton fiber.' 'Type O
  blood.' 'A loop fingerprint.' Lots of people match. Individual evidence points
  to *one* source: a fingerprint with all its little details matched up, a DNA
  profile, a specific nick in a shoe sole that matches a specific shoe. Here's the
  thing you have to know for Crime Busters: **almost everything you identify is
  class evidence.** You're not going to prove one person did it with a single
  test. You build the case by *stacking* — the soil matches, AND the fiber
  matches, AND the powder matches, and Suspect 3 is the only one all three point
  to.

  **Three: direct versus circumstantial evidence.** Direct evidence proves
  something by itself — a video of the crime, an eyewitness, a confession. You
  almost never get that in Crime Busters. Circumstantial evidence needs you to
  connect dots — 'her hair was on the victim, so she was probably there.' Now,
  people think 'circumstantial' means 'weak.' **It does not.** A pile of
  circumstantial evidence that all points the same way is a strong case. Most real
  convictions are built on circumstantial evidence. Don't ever write 'this is only
  circumstantial so it doesn't count.'

  **Four: presumptive versus confirmatory tests.** A presumptive test is fast and
  sensitive but not picky — it tells you what something *could* be. When iodine
  turns a powder black, that's presumptive for starch. When a powder fizzes with
  acid, that's presumptive for a carbonate. A confirmatory test nails the exact
  identity — DNA profiling, fancy lab instruments. **Everything you do in Crime
  Busters is presumptive plus comparison.** You're showing 'this behaves exactly
  like cornstarch, and it matches the flour on Suspect 2's apron.' You're not
  running a court-admissible confirmation, and that's fine — that's the event."

  **[ASK THE TEAM — one at a time, quick:]**
  - "Fingerprint pattern is 'loop.' Class or individual?" (Class.)
  - "Fingerprint with twelve matching minutiae in the same spots. Class or
    individual?" (Individual.)
  - "You have a suspect's motive but no physical evidence tying them to the
    scene. Strong or weak?" (Weak — motive without evidence.)
  - "Iodine turns the powder black. Have you *confirmed* it's cornstarch?" (No —
    presumptive. It could be flour, or baking powder. You need more tests, and a
    match to a suspect.)

- **[ACTIVITY: Chain of Custody in 90 seconds]**
  Hand one kid a "piece of evidence" (a labeled baggie with a cotton ball).
  Have it pass around the group; each person writes their name and the time on
  the bag before passing it. Then say: "Now imagine one person in the middle
  didn't sign. In court, the other side says 'how do we know nobody swapped it
  while it was unsigned?' and the evidence gets thrown out. **That's** chain of
  custody — the paper trail proving the evidence in court is the same evidence
  from the scene, untouched."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** Ask each kid to give you one class characteristic and one
  individual characteristic, out loud, no notes. If they can't, re-run the shoe
  example: "size and tread design = class; the exact cut in the heel = individual."

---

### 0:40–1:05 (25 min) — Main Activity: Work a Mini-Case Like a Grader

- **Goal:** kids practice pulling evidence out of a scenario, sorting it by
  suspect, and — critically — telling strong matches from common ones.

- **[ACTIVITY: The Mini-Case Packet]**
  Hand out the Mini-Case Packet (below). Read the scene aloud together. Then kids
  work in pairs (or solo if a 2-kid team) on **Part A: the Evidence Sorting
  Grid** — for each piece of evidence, which suspect(s) does it point to, and is
  it a **strong** match (unusual, specific) or a **common** match (lots of people
  fit)?

  Give them ~10 minutes, then go through it together on the whiteboard.

  Then **Part B: the Analysis Template** — as a group this first time, fill in the
  template on the whiteboard: culprit, motive, evidence-with-reasons for the
  culprit, then one sentence clearing each other suspect. Point out every place
  the grader hands out a point.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** After Part B, ask: "We said Dana had a strong motive. Did we
  accuse Dana? Why not?" (Motive without matching physical evidence isn't
  enough — you need both. The grader specifically looks for you noticing that.)

### 1:05–1:20 (15 min) — The Home Practice Kit + Safety

- **Goal:** every kid leaves with a kit and understands the rules; every family
  gets the safety agreement.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Qualitative analysis is half the test and the only way to get fast at it is
  reps. So here's a kit for each of you to keep at home.

  Inside: little labeled bags of powders — all food-grade, stuff from a kitchen —
  baking soda, cornstarch, salt, two kinds of sugar, Epsom salt, cream of tartar,
  citric acid, chalk, sand. A dropper bottle of iodine — the same weak stuff sold
  at the pharmacy for cuts. And a bottle of purple cabbage juice, which is a pH
  indicator — it's literally boiled cabbage water.

  From home you'll add: white vinegar, water, a magnet, some cups or a muffin
  tin, toothpicks.

  Here are the hard rules, and your parent signs a sheet saying they understand
  them:
  - **An adult is in the room every time. Always.**
  - **Goggles or glasses on.**
  - **Never taste anything** — ever, even though it's food. In the lab it's an
    'unknown' and we never taste unknowns. That habit matters at competition
    where the samples are *not* food.
  - **Smell by wafting** — wave the air toward your nose, never stick your nose
    in.
  - **No strong chemicals, no heat, no flames at home.** The strong acid — the
    HCl — you only ever use here, with me, in goggles. Vinegar is your home
    stand-in and it's plenty.
  - **Clean up completely** every time. Rinse everything, wipe the counter, wash
    your hands. Cleanup is scored at competition, so we practice it.

  The iodine stains brown — work on a paper towel, wear old clothes."

- **[ACTIVITY:]** Hand each kid their kit. Have them open it, check the contents
  against the list on the safety sheet, and read the ten rules out loud, taking
  turns. Send the **Home Lab Safety Agreement** home — it must come back signed
  before their first home lab (which is assigned after Session 2).

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** Ask: "What are the three things that never happen at home?"
  (Strong chemicals, heat, flames.) "What always happens?" (An adult in the room;
  goggles on; cleanup after.)

---

### 1:20–1:30 (10 min) — Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Recap of what a grader is looking for, because this is the lens for the whole
  season:
  1. **Locard** — every contact leaves a trace. That's why trace evidence exists.
  2. Almost everything you identify is **class evidence** — you win by *stacking*
     matches until one suspect fits all of them.
  3. **Circumstantial is not weak.** A stack of circumstantial evidence pointing
     one way is a strong case.
  4. Your tests are **presumptive plus comparison** — 'behaves like X and matches
     Suspect Y.'
  5. The **analysis writeup** is a quarter of the test and follows a structure:
     culprit, motive, evidence-with-reasons, then clear every other suspect."

- **Exit ticket** (say it out loud or on a slip; answers for you):
  1. **State Locard's Exchange Principle.** *(Every contact leaves a trace / when
     two objects touch, material transfers both ways.)*
  2. **Give one class characteristic and one individual characteristic of a
     shoe.** *(Class: size, tread pattern, brand. Individual: a specific cut,
     gouge, or wear mark.)*
  3. **A suspect has a strong motive but nothing physical ties them to the scene.
     Do you accuse them? Why or why not?** *(No — motive without physical
     evidence isn't enough; you note the motive but explain there's no
     supporting evidence.)*

- **Homework:** Homework 1 (paper only — the full analysis writeup for a fresh
  mini-case, plus an evidence-sorting exercise). Bring the signed safety
  agreement next time.

## The Mini-Case Packet (print for kids)

**The Scene.** At the community garden's Saturday plant sale, the cash box —
holding about $400 — is taken from under the folding table between 1:00 and 1:30
p.m. The table sits on a bare dirt path. Investigators recover: a few **coarse
reddish-tan soil** crumbs on the cash box lid; **three short white animal hairs**
on the tablecloth; a **blue cotton fiber** snagged on the table edge; a
**smear of white powder** on the cash box that **fizzes with vinegar** and is
**not fully water-soluble**; and a **partial fingerprint (loop pattern)** on the
metal latch.

**The Suspects.**
- **Alex** — runs the tomato-seedling booth two tables down. Wears a blue cotton
  work shirt. Has a white cat. Potting soil around the booth is dark and fine.
  Fingerprints: loop, loop. Was seen restocking seedlings at 1:15.
- **Blair** — the garden's volunteer coordinator, had a key to the cash box.
  Wears a green polyester vest. No pets. Was "walking the perimeter" alone from
  1:00 to 1:30. Fingerprints: whorl, loop.
- **Casey** — does the garden's masonry and path repair; there's a pile of
  **reddish crushed-limestone path gravel** by their truck, and a bag of
  **garden lime** (calcium carbonate). Wears a blue cotton jacket. Has a white
  dog. Fingerprints: loop, tented arch.
- **Dana** — was passed over for the coordinator job that Blair got, and has
  complained about it loudly. Runs the bake-sale table (flour, sugar, baking
  soda everywhere). Wears a red wool sweater. No pets. Fingerprints: whorl,
  whorl.

## Coach Answer Notes (do not hand out)

**Part A:**
- **Reddish-tan coarse soil** → **Casey** (reddish crushed-limestone path gravel
  by the truck). *Strong* — unusual, specific color and coarseness; the other
  suspects' soil is dark and fine.
- **Short white animal hair** → **Casey** (white dog) or **Alex** (white cat).
  *Common-ish* — need the hair ID (dog vs. cat) to split them. On its own,
  points at two people.
- **Blue cotton fiber** → **Alex** (blue cotton shirt) or **Casey** (blue cotton
  jacket). *Common* — blue cotton is everywhere.
- **White powder, fizzes with vinegar, not fully soluble** → **calcium carbonate
  (chalk / garden lime)** → **Casey** (has a bag of garden lime). *Strong* — the
  "fizzes AND not fully soluble" combination points specifically to a carbonate
  that isn't baking soda; Dana's baking soda would fizz but *would* fully
  dissolve. Good teaching moment about a partial result.
- **Loop fingerprint** → Alex, Casey, and Dana all have at least one loop; Blair
  and Dana… (Dana is whorl/whorl, so Dana is *excluded*). *Class evidence* —
  narrows to "not Blair, not Dana."

**Part B — worked:**
- **Culprit: Casey.** Motive isn't loud, but opportunity (path work gives them
  reason to be near the table) plus the physical stack.
- Evidence: the reddish path-gravel soil on the lid (matches the pile by Casey's
  truck); the garden-lime powder smear (Casey has a bag of it — carbonate that
  fizzes but doesn't fully dissolve); a white animal hair (Casey's white dog —
  confirm with hair ID); blue cotton fiber (Casey's jacket); loop print
  consistent with Casey (not exclusionary, but not contradicted).
- Timeline: no alibi given for Casey; Alex was seen restocking at 1:15.
- Clear the others:
  - **Alex** — blue cotton and a white pet match, but the soil and the carbonate
    powder don't; was seen working their booth at 1:15.
  - **Blair** — had a key and no alibi (weak spot!), but **zero** matching
    physical evidence, and the loop print excludes Blair's… actually Blair is
    whorl/loop so a loop print doesn't exclude Blair — note that the *lack of any
    trace evidence* is what clears Blair despite motive and opportunity.
  - **Dana** — strong, loud **motive** (passed over for the job), works with
    powders — but the powder at the scene is a *carbonate that doesn't dissolve*,
    not Dana's baking soda or flour; the wool sweater doesn't match the cotton
    fiber; and the fingerprint (whorl/whorl) **excludes** Dana. This is the
    teaching point: the suspect with the best motive is cleared by the physical
    evidence.

The lesson to name explicitly: **Dana looks guilty on motive; Casey is guilty on
evidence. Graders reward you for saying exactly that.**

## Coach Notes & Common Kid Mistakes

- **"Circumstantial = weak."** The most common misconception, and it costs points
  in the analysis. Drill: a stack of circumstantial evidence all pointing one
  direction is a *strong* case.
- **Accusing on motive alone.** Kids latch onto the suspect who's angry or
  jealous and stop reading. Force the habit: "what physical evidence ties this
  person to the scene?" If the answer is "none," they're not your culprit,
  however good the motive.
- **Ignoring the innocent suspects in the writeup.** Points are awarded for
  clearing each non-culprit. Kids write a paragraph about the culprit and stop.
- **Treating a partial result as a failed test.** The powder that fizzes *and*
  leaves residue isn't a "bad test" — it's telling you it's an insoluble
  carbonate (chalk/lime), not baking soda. Partial results are information.
- **Forgetting the kit rules by next week.** Re-ask the "never at home / always at
  home" questions at the start of Session 2.
