# Session 2: Powders 101 — The Workflow and the Chemistry
### Crime Busters Coach Script — [~150 min core, genuinely long — split across two meetings or cut hard, see below]

> **Coach heads-up: this session merges two original 90-min sessions into one meeting**, matching the co-coach's "Powders 101" deck, which teaches the powder-testing workflow and the underlying chemistry back to back. Nothing from either original session has been cut — this is long by design, not a mistake. If your slot is a hard 90 minutes, either run this across two meetings (Part 1 one week, Part 2 the next — this is what the two original sessions were before the merge, and splitting them back apart loses nothing) or use the "If You Only Have 90 Minutes" cut path below.

## Before You Start

- **Learning goals:** Each kid can (1) run the standard powder battery in the
  same order every time, using a pinch per test, (2) record an answer as
  *name + formula + reason*, (3) read a dichotomous key to an identification
  and build a simple key for a small set of powders, (4) give the chemical
  formula for the common powder set from memory, (5) explain *why* the
  iodine–starch test turns blue-black (an inclusion complex, not a reaction)
  and why carbonates fizz with acid, and (6) say what a reducing sugar is and
  predict the "backwards" iodine result with vitamin C.
- **Year 1 recap built in:** the team has a powder table from last year — Part
  1 turns it into a *procedure*; Part 2 explains *why* it works.
- **Materials:**
  - Knowledge Document Chapters 3 and 4. Read both before this session — Part
    2 especially is where the coach most needs to be ahead of the kids.
  - Per pair: a spot plate or white ice-cube tray, toothpicks, 2 cups of
    water, small dropper of iodine, small cup of white vinegar, pH paper or
    cabbage indicator, hand lens, paper towels.
  - **Known powders** for the Part 1 demo: baking soda, cornstarch, table
    salt, granulated sugar, chalk/calcium carbonate, gypsum. Label clearly.
  - **Unknown set A** — the same six powders, numbered cups (1–6), key
    written down.
  - **Unknown set B** — four powders for the key-building activity (suggest:
    Epsom salt, citric acid, cornstarch, salt).
  - A small amount of **1 M HCl** (competition strength) for the coach-only
    demo, with goggles and gloves, a tray, and a baking-soda sample.
  - For Part 2, additionally per pair: small labeled pinches of flour,
    powdered sugar, baking powder, vitamin C powder (crushed tablet is fine).
  - A cup of **vitamin C solution** (dissolve a 500 mg tablet in ~50 mL water)
    and a cup of dilute iodine for the decolorizing demo.
  - Printed **Results Grid**, **Dichotomous Key Worksheet**, **Formula Cards**
    (cut into name/formula pairs), and **Predict-Then-Test sheet** (all below).
  - Whiteboard.
  - Optional: a red cabbage, a pot — if you can heat a cornstarch-iodine
    mixture and show the color vanish and return on cooling, it's a great demo
    (coach does the heating).
- **Prep:** portion the known and unknown powders into labeled cups before
  kids arrive. Write your answer key for Unknown Set A. Make the vitamin C
  solution. Cut the formula cards. Print worksheets. One testing station per
  pair; goggles for everyone.
- **Vocabulary this session:** qualitative analysis · solubility · suspension
  · dichotomous key · reagent · carbonate · presumptive (recap) · amylose ·
  amylopectin · helix · charge-transfer complex · inclusion complex
  (clathrate) · carbonate / bicarbonate · reducing sugar · non-reducing sugar
  · reducing agent.

## Part 1: The Workflow

### 0:00–0:08 (8 min) — Recap & Hook: "Same Order, Every Time"

- **[ASK THE TEAM, quick, from Session 1:]** "What are the three things that
  never happen during a home lab?" (strong chemicals, heat, flames) "What
  always happens?" (adult present, goggles, cleanup). Collect signed safety
  agreements.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Last year you had a table of powders and their properties. The table is
  good. But at competition you'll have six or eight unknown cups and about
  fifteen minutes for all of them. The teams that finish do the **exact same
  tests in the exact same order** on every cup. No thinking about what to try
  next — just run the checklist. That's what today's Part 1 is: turning your
  table into a routine your hands can do without you. Then in Part 2 we get
  into *why* all of it works, and the exact chemical formulas."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Why does doing tests in a fixed order make you faster?"
  (No decisions between tests; you can set up all the samples and run one
  test across all of them at once; you don't forget a test.)

---

### 0:08–0:28 (20 min) — Direct Teaching: The Standard Powder Battery

- **Goal:** kids know the eight tests, in order, and what each one
  distinguishes.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Here's the battery. Write it on your note sheet — this exact order. Use a
  **pinch** each time, about what sticks to a wet toothpick. Work on the white
  tray so you can see color changes.

  **1. Look.** Hand lens. Is it a crystal like salt or sugar? A fine
  flour-like powder? Fibrous? What color, any sparkle?

  **2. Smell.** Waft it toward your nose. Most are odorless. Sodium acetate
  smells like vinegar once it's wet. Flour smells like flour.

  **3. Feel.** Rub a little between gloved fingers. Gritty means sugar or
  sand. Squeaky and silky means cornstarch. Soft and fine means baking soda or
  flour.

  **4. Water.** Pinch into about a milliliter of water and stir. Does it
  *completely* dissolve — clear solution — like salt, sugar, Epsom salt? Or
  stay cloudy — a suspension — like flour, chalk, gypsum? Cornstarch does a
  weird thing: it makes 'oobleck,' liquid when you stir slow, solid when you
  poke it fast.

  **5. pH of that water mix.** Dip pH paper or add two drops of cabbage
  indicator. Acidic — pink or red — means citric acid, cream of tartar,
  vitamin C. Near neutral — purple — means salt, sugar, cornstarch. Basic —
  blue or green — means baking soda, washing soda, sodium acetate.

  **6. Acid.** Fresh pinch, add a few drops of vinegar — or the HCl at
  competition. A strong fizz means a carbonate: baking soda, chalk, washing
  soda. A fizz *only after* you added water to a dry powder that already had
  acid in it means baking powder or Alka-Seltzer. Most powders: no fizz.

  **7. Iodine.** One drop on a *dry* pinch. Blue-black, almost black, means
  starch — cornstarch, flour, baking powder, and a little bit with powdered
  sugar. Stays orange-brown means no starch. And one weird one: it goes
  **colorless** with vitamin C. We'll cover why in Part 2.

  **8. Heat** — this one *I* do, not you. Tiny bit on foil over a flame. Sugar
  melts and turns brown and smells like caramel. Starch chars. Salt just
  crackles and pops and never melts. Carbonates just sit there."

- **[ACTIVITY: Watch One Full Run]**
  At the front, take **one known powder** — cornstarch works well — and
  narrate all eight tests out loud while the kids watch and write the results
  in their grid. Then do a second one — **baking soda** — faster, having them
  call out predictions before each test.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** Cover the whiteboard and ask the team to recite the eight
  tests in order. If they stall, give the memory hook: **look, smell, feel**
  (the free ones), then **water, pH, acid, iodine** (the reagent ones), then
  **heat** (coach only).

### 0:28–0:38 (10 min) — Recording an Answer That Scores

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "When you write an ID on the answer sheet, the grader is giving points for
  three separate things: the name, usually two points; the chemical formula,
  often two points; and each suspect you correctly implicate, a point each. So
  every entry looks like this:"

  Write on the board:
  > **Sample 3: Gypsum — CaSO₄·2H₂O — implicates Suspect 1 (does drywall
  > work)**

  "Name, formula, reason. If you write just 'gypsum' you might get two points
  and miss four. We'll drill the formulas in Part 2 — for now, know that the
  formula column exists and it's worth as much as the name."

- **[ACTIVITY:]** Give the team three fake result-lines and have them write
  the scoring answer format:
  - "Fizzes hard with acid, dissolves fully, pH 9." → *Baking soda — NaHCO₃ —
    implicates [whoever bakes].*
  - "Turns iodine black, won't dissolve, makes oobleck." → *Cornstarch —
    (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ — implicates [whoever cooks/does crafts].*
  - "Cubic crystals, dissolves fully, no fizz, no iodine, crackles on heat." →
    *Table salt — NaCl.*

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "What three things go in every ID answer?" (name, formula,
  reason/suspect)

---

### 0:38–0:48 (10 min) — Dichotomous Keys

- **Goal:** kids can follow a branching key and see why it's fast.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "A dichotomous key is a set of either/or questions. Each question splits
  what's left roughly in half, so six unknowns take about three questions
  each instead of eight tests each. Here's a key for our core six."

  Put this on the board (it's also in the Knowledge Document, Appendix D):
  ```
  1. Fizzes strongly with acid?   yes → 2      no → 3
  2. Dissolves fully in water?     yes → BAKING SODA (NaHCO3)
                                   no  → CHALK / calcium carbonate (CaCO3)
  3. Blue-black with iodine?       yes → CORNSTARCH ((C6H10O5)n)
                                   no  → 4
  4. Dissolves fully in water?     no  → GYPSUM (CaSO4·2H2O)
                                   yes → 5
  5. Cubic crystals, no melt on heat?  yes → TABLE SALT (NaCl)
                                       no  → SUGAR (C12H22O11)
  ```

- **[ACTIVITY: Read the Key]**
  Give each kid the **Dichotomous Key Worksheet** — three described powders —
  and have them trace the key to the answer, writing which numbered steps
  they used.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "In this key, which single test separates the most
  powders at once?" (The acid-fizz test — it immediately isolates the two
  carbonates.)

### 0:48–1:05 (17 min) — Main Activity: Run the Core Set (Unknown Set A)

- **Goal:** each pair correctly identifies all six numbered unknowns, working
  the battery and/or the key, recording name + formula + a plausible reason.

- **[ACTIVITY: Timed Core-Set Run]**
  Each pair gets numbered cups 1–6 (your shuffled core set), a testing
  station, and a blank Results Grid. Rules:
  - Goggles on.
  - One partner tests, one records, then swap halfway.
  - Set up all six water-solubility tests first, *then* go down the line with
    iodine, *then* acid — batch the tests, don't finish one cup before
    starting the next.
  - Call "done" when all six are named with formulas.

  Give them ~12 minutes. Note each pair's time. Then reveal the key and have
  them check. For any miss, have them tell you which test result they
  misread.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** Spot-check understanding, not just answers: point at their
  "chalk" and ask "what two results together told you chalk and not baking
  soda?" (Both fizz, but chalk did **not** fully dissolve.)

---

### 1:05–1:20 (15 min) — Build Your Own Key (Unknown Set B)

- **Goal:** kids design a key, which forces them to think about which test
  splits a set most efficiently.

- **[ACTIVITY: Key-Building Challenge]**
  Tell them Set B contains four powders: **Epsom salt, citric acid,
  cornstarch, table salt** (they know the list; the cups are unlabeled). In
  pairs, they:
  1. Run whatever tests they need to tell the four apart.
  2. Write a dichotomous key — first question, branches, down to all four.
  3. Trade keys with the other pair (or with you) and test-drive it on the
     cups.

  A good key here starts with iodine (isolates cornstarch), then pH
  (isolates citric acid), then a distinguishing test for Epsom salt vs. salt
  (crystal shape — Epsom salt is needle-like and bitter; salt is cubic).

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Why is starting with the iodine test smart for this
  set?" (Only one of the four has starch, so one question removes cornstarch
  cleanly.)

### 1:20–1:26 (6 min) — Coach Demo: 1 M HCl on a Carbonate

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "At competition the acid you use is one molar hydrochloric acid — much
  stronger than vinegar. You'll use it, but with goggles down and gloves on,
  over the tray, a couple of drops at a time, cap on between uses. Watch."

  With goggles and gloves, put a pinch of baking soda in the tray and add a
  few drops of 1 M HCl. Point out: the fizz is faster and more vigorous than
  with vinegar. "Same reaction — carbonate plus acid makes carbon dioxide gas
  — just faster because the acid is stronger. If any splashes on you: rinse
  with lots of water and tell the supervisor."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Where do you use HCl — home or here?" (Only here, with
  the coach.) "What's the home stand-in?" (Vinegar.)

## Part 2: The Chemistry

### 0:00–0:06 (6 min) — Recap & Hook: "Now the 'Why'"

- **[ASK THE TEAM:]** "Run me the powder battery, in order." (look, smell,
  feel, water, pH, acid, iodine, heat) "What does iodine turning black tell
  you?" (starch present)

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Part 1: what tests to run. Part 2: *why they work* and *what the exact
  formulas are* — because the harder questions ask both. A real question
  we're going to be able to answer by the end of today is: 'Explain why flour
  turns black in iodine.' Not 'it has starch' — the full reason, worth four
  points."

- **Coach note — the official list.** The rules name exactly **13 solids**
  that can appear: anhydrous sodium acetate, yeast, vitamin C, calcium
  carbonate, table salt, sugar, flour, gypsum, cornstarch, baking soda,
  gelatin, Alka-Seltzer, white sand. Make sure the team knows all 13 cold.
  Invitationals often add off-list powders (Epsom salt, cream of tartar,
  citric acid, MSG, washing soda) — we practice those too because the
  *method* is the same, but the 13 are the ones guaranteed to show up.

---

### 0:06–0:24 (18 min) — Direct Teaching: The Formulas

- **Goal:** kids can produce formulas for the core powder set.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "You already know the names. Now pair each with its formula. I'll group
  them so they're easier to remember."

  Build this on the board in groups, explaining the logic:

  **The carbonates (all fizz with acid):**
  - Baking soda = sodium bicarbonate = **NaHCO₃**
  - Washing soda = sodium carbonate = **Na₂CO₃** (no H — that's the
    difference)
  - Chalk / limestone = calcium carbonate = **CaCO₃**

  **The sugars and starch (carbon, hydrogen, oxygen only):**
  - Table sugar = sucrose = **C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁**
  - Starch (cornstarch) = **(C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ** — the little *n* means "this unit,
    repeated many times." It's a polymer of glucose.

  **The salts:**
  - Table salt = sodium chloride = **NaCl**
  - Epsom salt = magnesium sulfate = **MgSO₄·7H₂O** (the ·7H₂O = 7 waters in
    the crystal)
  - Gypsum = calcium sulfate dihydrate = **CaSO₄·2H₂O**

  **The acids (sour, low pH):**
  - Citric acid = **C₆H₈O₇**
  - Vitamin C = ascorbic acid = **C₆H₈O₆** — *one less oxygen than citric
    acid, and it's the one that decolorizes iodine*
  - Cream of tartar = potassium bitartrate = **KHC₄H₄O₆**

  **The odd ones:**
  - Sand = silicon dioxide = **SiO₂**
  - Sodium acetate = **NaC₂H₃O₂**

- **[ACTIVITY: Formula Matching Race]**
  Lay out the cut Formula Cards (names in one pile, formulas in another).
  Kids race — solo or in pairs — to pair them all up. Shuffle and repeat,
  faster. Then remove the formula cards and have them write the formulas from
  the names.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** Quick oral quiz: "Baking soda?" (NaHCO₃) "Sucrose?"
  (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) "Gypsum?" (CaSO₄·2H₂O) "Sand?" (SiO₂) "The vitamin C one?"
  (C₆H₈O₆)

### 0:24–0:44 (20 min) — Direct Teaching: Why the Iodine Test Works

- **Goal:** kids can explain the mechanism well enough to earn a 4-point
  short-answer.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Starch is made of two kinds of glucose chains. **Amylose** is long and
  straight. **Amylopectin** is branched, like a bush. In water, the straight
  amylose chains coil up into a spring shape — a **helix**.

  The iodine in the reagent isn't just I₂. In solution it's mostly
  **triiodide** — three iodine atoms in a row, I₃⁻ — and longer chains of
  iodine. Those long straight iodine chains slide right **inside** the
  amylose helix, like a wire through a spring.

  Once the iodine is trapped inside, the iodine and the starch **share
  electrons loosely** — chemists call it a **charge-transfer complex**. That
  shared-electron arrangement absorbs almost all the visible light that hits
  it, so it looks **blue-black**.

  Three things to know for full points:
  - It is **not a chemical reaction**. No bonds break, nothing new is made.
    The iodine is just physically **caged inside** the starch helix. The word
    for one molecule caged in another is an **inclusion complex**, or a
    *clathrate*.
  - **Amylose gives blue-black. Amylopectin and glycogen give red-brown** —
    their branches are too short to hold a long iodine chain.
  - **Heat makes the color disappear** — the helix uncoils when it's warm,
    the iodine falls out — and the color **comes back when it cools** and the
    helix re-forms.
  - **Plain sugars — sucrose, glucose — give no color.** No helix, nothing to
    trap the iodine."

- **[DEMO — coach does any heating:]**
  Make a cornstarch-water paste, add a drop of iodine → deep blue-black. If
  you can heat it (double boiler, microwave in short bursts): the color fades
  to pale/orange. Let it cool → blue-black returns. Narrate: "the helix
  opened, the iodine left; it cooled, the helix closed, the iodine went back
  in."

- **[ACTIVITY: Answer the 4-Point Question]**
  On paper, each kid writes their own answer to: *"Explain why and what makes
  flour turn black in iodine. (4 points)"* Then read two or three aloud and
  polish them together against this model:
  > Flour contains **starch**. The starch's **amylose** molecules coil into a
  > **helix** (1). **Iodine (as triiodide ions)** slots **inside** the helix
  > (1). The trapped iodine and the starch form a **charge-transfer complex**
  > that absorbs visible light (1), which makes the mixture look
  > **blue-black** (1). It is an **inclusion complex**, not a chemical
  > reaction.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Is the iodine test a chemical reaction? What's the right
  word?" (No — an inclusion complex / clathrate; the iodine is trapped inside
  the amylose helix.)

### 0:44–0:52 (8 min) — Direct Teaching: Why Carbonates Fizz

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "The fizz test. A carbonate or bicarbonate plus an acid releases **carbon
  dioxide gas** — that's the bubbles.

  Word equation for baking soda and vinegar:
  **sodium bicarbonate + acetic acid → sodium acetate + water + carbon
  dioxide**

  Symbol equation: **NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → NaC₂H₃O₂ + H₂O + CO₂↑**

  The little up-arrow means a gas leaves. Because the gas escapes, the
  reaction keeps going until a reactant runs out — it doesn't reach a
  stopping balance.

  Chalk — calcium carbonate — fizzes the same way, but it's not soluble, so
  the acid can only reach the surface of each grain. The fizz is slower and
  you're left with cloudy undissolved chalk. That slower, partial fizz is
  actually a *clue* — it says 'carbonate, but not baking soda.'"

- **[ACTIVITY:]** Kids test baking soda vs. chalk with vinegar side by side
  and describe the difference in words for their notes.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "What gas makes the fizz?" (carbon dioxide) "Why is the
  chalk fizz slower?" (it's insoluble — acid only reaches the grain surface)

---

### 0:52–1:02 (10 min) — Reducing Sugars & the Benedict's Test

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "One more test you won't run — the reagent isn't supplied — but it's a
  short-answer question. **Benedict's solution** is blue. When you heat it
  with a **reducing sugar** — glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose — it turns
  **green, then yellow, then brick-red**. Those sugars have a chemical group
  that can 'reduce' the copper in the blue solution, dropping out a
  red-orange solid.

  **Sucrose — table sugar — and starch are *non-reducing*.** They stay blue,
  unless you first break them down into their smaller sugars.

  So if a question says 'Benedict's turned red,' the answer is 'a reducing
  sugar is present.' If it stayed blue with table sugar, that's expected —
  sucrose is non-reducing."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Benedict's turns brick-red. What does that mean?" (a
  reducing sugar is present) "Does table sugar do that?" (no — sucrose is
  non-reducing)

---

### 1:02–1:18 (16 min) — Main Activity: Predict, Then Test the Iodine Panel

- **Goal:** kids apply the mechanism to predict results, including the tricky
  ones (powdered sugar, baking powder, vitamin C).

- **[ACTIVITY: Predict-Then-Test]**
  Hand out the Predict-Then-Test sheet. Each pair has labeled pinches of:
  cornstarch, flour, granulated sugar, **powdered sugar**, **baking powder**,
  table salt, and a cup of **vitamin C solution**.

  Step 1 — **before testing**, they write a prediction for each: will iodine
  go blue-black, stay orange, or do something else? And *why*, in a few
  words.

  Step 2 — test each with one drop of iodine. For the vitamin C, add a drop
  of **dilute iodine to the vitamin C solution** and watch.

  Step 3 — explain any surprises.

  Expected results:
  - cornstarch, flour → **blue-black** (starch)
  - granulated sugar, salt → **stays orange** (no starch)
  - **powdered sugar → faint blue** — it has ~3% cornstarch added as
    anti-caking
  - **baking powder → blue-black** — it contains cornstarch (this is how you
    tell it from plain baking soda!)
  - **vitamin C solution → iodine goes colorless/clear** — vitamin C is a
    **reducing agent**; it chemically converts the iodine to colorless
    iodide, so there's no iodine left to make a color. This one *is* a real
    reaction, unlike the starch test.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Powdered sugar gave a faint blue. Why, if sugar has no
  starch?" (cornstarch is added to powdered sugar to keep it from clumping)
  "Baking powder went black but baking soda doesn't. What's that telling
  you?" (baking powder contains cornstarch — the iodine result separates the
  two)

## Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Today, both halves: the routine — **look, smell, feel, water, pH, acid,
  iodine, heat**, same order every cup, a dichotomous key as the fast path —
  and the chemistry underneath it. Iodine + starch = the amylose helix traps
  iodine, forms a charge-transfer complex, absorbs light, looks blue-black —
  an inclusion complex, not a reaction. Carbonate + acid = CO₂ gas. Vitamin C
  decolorizes iodine because it's a reducing agent — that one *is* a
  reaction."

- **Exit ticket:**
  1. **List the powder battery in order.** *(look, smell, feel, water
     solubility, pH, acid reaction, iodine, heat)*
  2. **Two powders both fizz with acid. One dissolves fully, one stays
     cloudy. Which is which?** *(Dissolves = baking soda NaHCO₃; cloudy =
     calcium carbonate / chalk CaCO₃)*
  3. **What are the three parts of an ID answer that scores full points?**
     *(name, chemical formula, the suspect it implicates + why)*
  4. **Give the formula for baking soda, sucrose, and gypsum.** *(NaHCO₃;
     C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁; CaSO₄·2H₂O)*
  5. **In one or two sentences, why does starch turn blue-black with
     iodine?** *(Amylose coils into a helix; iodine slides inside; the
     trapped iodine forms a charge-transfer complex that absorbs light →
     blue-black; it's an inclusion complex, not a reaction.)*
  6. **Baking soda and baking powder both fizz. One test tells them apart —
     which, and how?** *(Iodine — baking powder contains cornstarch so it
     turns blue-black; baking soda alone does not.)*

- **Homework:** Homework 2 — paper track (workflow + key-building + formula
  matching + test-mechanism short answers) + **Home Lab 2** (the powder
  workflow on 4 knowns at home, with a parent) and **Home Lab 3** (starch and
  carbonate tests at home). Only assign the home labs to kids whose safety
  agreement is signed and returned.

## Coach Notes & Common Kid Mistakes

**Part 1 (the workflow):**
- **Using too much sample.** A heaping scoop wastes material and makes
  solubility hard to read. A pinch. Model it.
- **Finishing one cup before starting the next.** Slow. Teach batching: all
  the water tests, then all the iodine, then all the acid.
- **Reading "cloudy" as "dissolved."** Chalk and gypsum make a cloudy
  suspension that can look milky-dissolved. Have them wait 20 seconds and
  look for settling.
- **Iodine on a wet sample.** The color is clearest on a dry pinch with one
  drop. A watery smear dilutes it.
- **Skipping the formula.** They'll want to write just the name. Every rep,
  make them say the formula too — it builds the recall they'll need in Part
  2.
- **Forgetting goggles for the "safe" powders.** Habit matters. Goggles on
  before any cup is opened, home or here.

**Part 2 (the chemistry):**
- **"The iodine test is a chemical reaction."** The single most common wrong
  answer. It's an *inclusion complex* — iodine physically trapped in the
  helix. (The vitamin C decolorizing, by contrast, *is* a reaction — don't
  let that blur the point.)
- **Confusing amylose and amylopectin.** Amylose = straight = blue-black.
  Amylopectin = branched = red-brown. Mnemonic: "amyl**ose** is a r**ose** on
  a straight stem."
- **Writing NaHCO₃ for washing soda.** Washing soda (sodium carbonate) is
  Na₂CO₃ — no hydrogen. Baking soda (bicarbonate) has the H.
- **Forgetting the *n* in the starch formula.** (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ — it's a polymer.
  Same for cellulose/cotton.
- **Expecting vitamin C to turn iodine black because it's a "powder like the
  others."** It has no starch; instead it *removes* the iodine color
  entirely. This trips kids every year.
- **Powdered sugar surprise.** Kids assume "sugar = no iodine" and mark it
  wrong. The added cornstarch gives a real faint blue.
