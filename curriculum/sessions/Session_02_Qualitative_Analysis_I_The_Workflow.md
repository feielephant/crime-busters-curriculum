# Session 2: Qualitative Analysis I — The Workflow
### Crime Busters Coach Script — [~90 min]

## Before You Start

- **Learning goals:** Each kid can (1) run the standard powder battery in the same
  order every time, using a pinch per test, (2) record an answer as
  *name + formula + reason*, (3) read a dichotomous key to an identification, and
  (4) build a simple key for a small set of powders. They should be able to
  correctly ID the 6-powder core set (baking soda, chalk, cornstarch, salt,
  sugar, gypsum) as a timed pair.
- **Year 1 recap built in:** the team has a powder table from last year — we're
  turning it into a *procedure*.
- **Materials:**
  - Knowledge Document Chapter 3 (and the powder table in Ch. 4). Read first.
  - Per pair: a spot plate or white ice-cube tray, toothpicks, 2 cups of water,
    small dropper of iodine, small cup of white vinegar, pH paper or cabbage
    indicator, hand lens, paper towels.
  - **Known powders** for the demo: baking soda, cornstarch, table salt,
    granulated sugar, chalk/calcium carbonate, gypsum (plaster of Paris powder or
    drywall dust). Label these clearly.
  - **Unknown set A** — the same six powders in numbered cups (1–6), your key
    written down.
  - **Unknown set B** — four powders for the key-building activity (suggest:
    Epsom salt, citric acid, cornstarch, salt).
  - A small amount of **1 M HCl** (competition strength) for the coach-only demo,
    with goggles and gloves, a tray, and a baking-soda sample.
  - Printed **Results Grid** and **Dichotomous Key Worksheet** (below).
- **Prep:** portion the known and unknown powders into labeled cups before kids
  arrive. Write your answer key for Unknown Set A. Set up one testing station per
  pair. Have goggles for everyone.
- **Vocabulary:** qualitative analysis · solubility · suspension · dichotomous
  key · reagent · carbonate · presumptive (recap).

> **If you only have 60 minutes:** Cut the key-building activity (1:05–1:20) and
> assign it as part of Homework 2. Do the core-set run (Unknown Set A) but with
> four powders instead of six (drop gypsum and chalk; keep baking soda, corn
> starch, salt, sugar). Keep the HCl safety demo.

## Segment-by-Segment Script

### 0:00–0:08 (8 min) — Recap & Hook: "Same Order, Every Time"

- **[ASK THE TEAM, quick, from last week:]** "What are the three things that
  never happen during a home lab?" (strong chemicals, heat, flames) "What always
  happens?" (adult present, goggles, cleanup). Collect signed safety agreements.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Last year you had a table of powders and their properties. The table is good.
  But at competition you'll have six or eight unknown cups and about fifteen
  minutes for all of them. The teams that finish do the **exact same tests in the
  exact same order** on every cup. No thinking about what to try next — just run
  the checklist. That's what today is: turning your table into a routine your
  hands can do without you."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Why does doing tests in a fixed order make you faster?"
  (No decisions between tests; you can set up all the samples and run one test
  across all of them at once; you don't forget a test.)

---

### 0:08–0:28 (20 min) — Direct Teaching: The Standard Powder Battery

- **Goal:** kids know the eight tests, in order, and what each one distinguishes.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Here's the battery. Write it on your note sheet — this exact order. Use a
  **pinch** each time, about what sticks to a wet toothpick. Work on the white
  tray so you can see color changes.

  **1. Look.** Hand lens. Is it a crystal like salt or sugar? A fine flour-like
  powder? Fibrous? What color, any sparkle?

  **2. Smell.** Waft it toward your nose. Most are odorless. Sodium acetate
  smells like vinegar once it's wet. Flour smells like flour.

  **3. Feel.** Rub a little between gloved fingers. Gritty means sugar or sand.
  Squeaky and silky means cornstarch. Soft and fine means baking soda or flour.

  **4. Water.** Pinch into about a milliliter of water and stir. Does it
  *completely* dissolve — clear solution — like salt, sugar, Epsom salt? Or stay
  cloudy — a suspension — like flour, chalk, gypsum? Cornstarch does a weird
  thing: it makes 'oobleck,' liquid when you stir slow, solid when you poke it
  fast.

  **5. pH of that water mix.** Dip pH paper or add two drops of cabbage
  indicator. Acidic — pink or red — means citric acid, cream of tartar, vitamin
  C. Near neutral — purple — means salt, sugar, cornstarch. Basic — blue or green
  — means baking soda, washing soda, sodium acetate.

  **6. Acid.** Fresh pinch, add a few drops of vinegar — or the HCl at
  competition. A strong fizz means a carbonate: baking soda, chalk, washing soda.
  A fizz *only after* you added water to a dry powder that already had acid in it
  means baking powder or Alka-Seltzer. Most powders: no fizz.

  **7. Iodine.** One drop on a *dry* pinch. Blue-black, almost black, means
  starch — cornstarch, flour, baking powder, and a little bit with powdered
  sugar. Stays orange-brown means no starch. And one weird one: it goes
  **colorless** with vitamin C. We'll cover why next week.

  **8. Heat** — this one *I* do, not you. Tiny bit on foil over a flame. Sugar
  melts and turns brown and smells like caramel. Starch chars. Salt just
  crackles and pops and never melts. Carbonates just sit there."

- **[ACTIVITY: Watch One Full Run]**
  At the front, take **one known powder** — cornstarch works well — and narrate
  all eight tests out loud while the kids watch and write the results in their
  grid. Then do a second one — **baking soda** — faster, having them call out
  predictions before each test.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** Cover the whiteboard and ask the team to recite the eight
  tests in order. If they stall, give the memory hook: **look, smell, feel** (the
  free ones), then **water, pH, acid, iodine** (the reagent ones), then **heat**
  (coach only).

---

### 0:28–0:38 (10 min) — Recording an Answer That Scores

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "When you write an ID on the answer sheet, the grader is giving points for
  three separate things: the name, usually two points; the chemical formula,
  often two points; and each suspect you correctly implicate, a point each. So
  every entry looks like this:"

  Write on the board:
  > **Sample 3: Gypsum — CaSO₄·2H₂O — implicates Suspect 1 (does drywall work)**

  "Name, formula, reason. If you write just 'gypsum' you might get two points and
  miss four. We'll drill the formulas next week — for now, know that the formula
  column exists and it's worth as much as the name."

- **[ACTIVITY:]** Give the team three fake result-lines and have them write the
  scoring answer format:
  - "Fizzes hard with acid, dissolves fully, pH 9." → *Baking soda — NaHCO₃ —
    implicates [whoever bakes].*
  - "Turns iodine black, won't dissolve, makes oobleck." → *Cornstarch —
    (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ — implicates [whoever cooks/does crafts].*
  - "Cubic crystals, dissolves fully, no fizz, no iodine, crackles on heat." →
    *Table salt — NaCl.*

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "What three things go in every ID answer?" (name, formula,
  reason/suspect)

---

### 0:38–0:48 (10 min) — Dichotomous Keys

- **Goal:** kids can follow a branching key and see why it's fast.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "A dichotomous key is a set of either/or questions. Each question splits what's
  left roughly in half, so six unknowns take about three questions each instead of
  eight tests each. Here's a key for our core six."

  Put this on the board (it's also in the Knowledge Document, Appendix D):
  ```
  1. Fizzes strongly with acid?   yes → 2      no → 3
  2. Dissolves fully in water?     yes → BAKING SODA (NaHCO3)
                                   no  → CHALK / calcium carbonate (CaCO3)
  3. Blue-black with iodine?       yes → CORNSTARCH ((C6H10O5)n)
                                   no  → 4
  4. Dissolves fully in water?     no  → GYPSUM (CaSO4·2H2O)
                                   yes → 5
  5. Cubic crystals, no melt on heat?  yes → TABLE SALT (NaCl)
                                       no  → SUGAR (C12H22O11)
  ```

- **[ACTIVITY: Read the Key]**
  Give each kid the **Dichotomous Key Worksheet** — three described powders — and
  have them trace the key to the answer, writing which numbered steps they used.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "In this key, which single test separates the most powders at
  once?" (The acid-fizz test — it immediately isolates the two carbonates.)

---

### 0:48–1:05 (17 min) — Main Activity: Run the Core Set (Unknown Set A)

- **Goal:** each pair correctly identifies all six numbered unknowns, working the
  battery and/or the key, recording name + formula + a plausible reason.

- **[ACTIVITY: Timed Core-Set Run]**
  Each pair gets numbered cups 1–6 (your shuffled core set), a testing station,
  and a blank Results Grid. Rules:
  - Goggles on.
  - One partner tests, one records, then swap halfway.
  - Set up all six water-solubility tests first, *then* go down the line with
    iodine, *then* acid — batch the tests, don't finish one cup before starting
    the next.
  - Call "done" when all six are named with formulas.

  Give them ~12 minutes. Note each pair's time. Then reveal the key and have them
  check. For any miss, have them tell you which test result they misread.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** Spot-check understanding, not just answers: point at their
  "chalk" and ask "what two results together told you chalk and not baking soda?"
  (Both fizz, but chalk did **not** fully dissolve.)

---

### 1:05–1:20 (15 min) — Build Your Own Key (Unknown Set B)

- **Goal:** kids design a key, which forces them to think about which test splits
  a set most efficiently.

- **[ACTIVITY: Key-Building Challenge]**
  Tell them Set B contains four powders: **Epsom salt, citric acid, cornstarch,
  table salt** (they know the list; the cups are unlabeled). In pairs, they:
  1. Run whatever tests they need to tell the four apart.
  2. Write a dichotomous key — first question, branches, down to all four.
  3. Trade keys with the other pair (or with you) and test-drive it on the cups.

  A good key here starts with iodine (isolates cornstarch), then pH (isolates
  citric acid), then a distinguishing test for Epsom salt vs. salt (crystal shape
  — Epsom salt is needle-like and bitter; salt is cubic).

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Why is starting with the iodine test smart for this set?"
  (Only one of the four has starch, so one question removes cornstarch cleanly.)

---

### 1:20–1:26 (6 min) — Coach Demo: 1 M HCl on a Carbonate

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "At competition the acid you use is one molar hydrochloric acid — much stronger
  than vinegar. You'll use it, but with goggles down and gloves on, over the
  tray, a couple of drops at a time, cap on between uses. Watch."

  With goggles and gloves, put a pinch of baking soda in the tray and add a few
  drops of 1 M HCl. Point out: the fizz is faster and more vigorous than with
  vinegar. "Same reaction — carbonate plus acid makes carbon dioxide gas — just
  faster because the acid is stronger. If any splashes on you: rinse with lots of
  water and tell the supervisor."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Where do you use HCl — home or here?" (Only here, with the
  coach.) "What's the home stand-in?" (Vinegar.)

---

### 1:26–1:30 (4 min) — Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "The routine: **look, smell, feel, water, pH, acid, iodine, heat** — same order
  every cup, batch the tests, record name plus formula plus reason. A dichotomous
  key is the fast path through a whole bench."

- **Exit ticket:**
  1. **List the powder battery in order.** *(look, smell, feel, water solubility,
     pH, acid reaction, iodine, heat)*
  2. **Two powders both fizz with acid. One dissolves fully, one stays cloudy.
     Which is which?** *(Dissolves = baking soda NaHCO₃; cloudy = calcium
     carbonate / chalk CaCO₃)*
  3. **What are the three parts of an ID answer that scores full points?**
     *(name, chemical formula, the suspect it implicates + why)*

- **Homework:** Homework 2 — paper track (reading and building keys) + **Home Lab
  2** (the powder workflow on 4 knowns at home, with a parent). Only assign the
  home lab to kids whose safety agreement is signed and returned.

## Dichotomous Key Worksheet (print for kids)

Trace the core-set key. Write the step numbers you pass through, then the answer.

1. **Powder A:** stays cloudy in water; does not fizz with acid; turns iodine
   blue-black. → steps: ____ → **_______________**
2. **Powder B:** fizzes hard with acid; dissolves completely; pH about 9.
   → steps: ____ → **_______________**
3. **Powder C:** dissolves completely; no fizz; no iodine color; forms cubic
   crystals; does not melt on gentle heat. → steps: ____ → **_______________**

*(Answers: A = cornstarch [1no → 3yes]; B = baking soda [1yes → 2yes];
C = table salt [1no → 3no → 4yes → 5yes].)*

## Coach Notes & Common Kid Mistakes

- **Using too much sample.** A heaping scoop wastes material and makes solubility
  hard to read. A pinch. Model it.
- **Finishing one cup before starting the next.** Slow. Teach batching: all the
  water tests, then all the iodine, then all the acid.
- **Reading "cloudy" as "dissolved."** Chalk and gypsum make a cloudy suspension
  that can look milky-dissolved. Have them wait 20 seconds and look for settling.
- **Iodine on a wet sample.** The color is clearest on a dry pinch with one
  drop. A watery smear dilutes it.
- **Skipping the formula.** They'll want to write just the name. Every rep,
  make them say the formula too — it builds the recall they'll need next week.
- **Forgetting goggles for the "safe" powders.** Habit matters. Goggles on before
  any cup is opened, home or here.
