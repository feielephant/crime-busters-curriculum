# Session 3: Qualitative Analysis II — The Chemistry
### Crime Busters Coach Script — [~90 min, dense — do not add to it]

## Before You Start

- **Learning goals:** Each kid can (1) give the chemical formula for the common
  powder set from memory, (2) explain *why* the iodine–starch test turns
  blue-black (amylose helix traps iodine → charge-transfer complex → absorbs
  light; it's an inclusion complex, not a reaction), (3) explain why carbonates
  fizz with acid (CO₂ gas released) and write the word equation, (4) say what a
  reducing sugar is and what Benedict's solution shows, and (5) predict and
  explain the "backwards" iodine result with vitamin C.
- **Year 1 recap built in:** the powder battery from Session 2; last year's
  powder table.
- **Materials:**
  - Knowledge Document Chapter 4. Read it closely before this session — this is
    the session where the coach most needs to be ahead of the kids.
  - Per pair: iodine dropper, spot plate, toothpicks, water, and small labeled
    pinches of: **cornstarch, flour, powdered sugar, granulated sugar, baking
    powder, vitamin C powder (crushed tablet is fine), table salt.**
  - A cup of **vitamin C solution** (dissolve a 500 mg tablet in ~50 mL water)
    and a cup of **dilute iodine** for the decolorizing demo.
  - Printed **Formula Cards** for the matching game (cut into name/formula pairs)
    and the **Predict-Then-Test sheet** (below).
  - Whiteboard.
  - Optional: a red cabbage, a pot — if you can heat some cornstarch-iodine
    mixture and show the color vanish and return on cooling, it's a great demo
    (coach does the heating).
- **Prep:** make the vitamin C solution; portion the powders; cut the formula
  cards; print worksheets.
- **Vocabulary:** amylose · amylopectin · helix · charge-transfer complex ·
  inclusion complex (clathrate) · carbonate / bicarbonate · reducing sugar ·
  non-reducing sugar · reducing agent.

> **If you only have 60 minutes:** Cut the Benedict's / reducing-sugar teaching
> (0:52–1:02) — mention it in one sentence and put it on the quiz reading. Keep
> everything else; the iodine mechanism and the formulas are the core of this
> session.

## Segment-by-Segment Script

### 0:00–0:06 (6 min) — Recap & Hook: "Now the 'Why'"

- **[ASK THE TEAM:]** "Run me the powder battery, in order." (look, smell, feel,
  water, pH, acid, iodine, heat) "What does iodine turning black tell you?"
  (starch present)

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Last week: what tests to run. This week: *why they work* and *what the exact
  formulas are* — because the harder tests ask both. A real question we're going
  to be able to answer by the end of today is: 'Explain why flour turns black in
  iodine.' Not 'it has starch' — the full reason, worth four points."

---

### 0:06–0:24 (18 min) — Direct Teaching: The Formulas

- **Goal:** kids can produce formulas for the core powder set.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "You already know the names. Now pair each with its formula. I'll group them so
  they're easier to remember."

  Build this on the board in groups, explaining the logic:

  **The carbonates (all fizz with acid):**
  - Baking soda = sodium bicarbonate = **NaHCO₃**
  - Washing soda = sodium carbonate = **Na₂CO₃** (no H — that's the difference)
  - Chalk / limestone = calcium carbonate = **CaCO₃**

  **The sugars and starch (carbon, hydrogen, oxygen only):**
  - Table sugar = sucrose = **C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁**
  - Starch (cornstarch) = **(C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ** — the little *n* means "this unit,
    repeated many times." It's a polymer of glucose.

  **The salts:**
  - Table salt = sodium chloride = **NaCl**
  - Epsom salt = magnesium sulfate = **MgSO₄·7H₂O** (the ·7H₂O = 7 waters in the
    crystal)
  - Gypsum = calcium sulfate dihydrate = **CaSO₄·2H₂O**

  **The acids (sour, low pH):**
  - Citric acid = **C₆H₈O₇**
  - Vitamin C = ascorbic acid = **C₆H₈O₆** — *one less oxygen than citric acid,
    and it's the one that decolorizes iodine*
  - Cream of tartar = potassium bitartrate = **KHC₄H₄O₆**

  **The odd ones:**
  - Sand = silicon dioxide = **SiO₂**
  - Sodium acetate = **NaC₂H₃O₂**

- **[ACTIVITY: Formula Matching Race]**
  Lay out the cut Formula Cards (names in one pile, formulas in another). Kids
  race — solo or in pairs — to pair them all up. Shuffle and repeat, faster.
  Then remove the formula cards and have them write the formulas from the names.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** Quick oral quiz: "Baking soda?" (NaHCO₃) "Sucrose?"
  (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) "Gypsum?" (CaSO₄·2H₂O) "Sand?" (SiO₂) "The vitamin C one?"
  (C₆H₈O₆)

---

### 0:24–0:44 (20 min) — Direct Teaching: Why the Iodine Test Works

- **Goal:** kids can explain the mechanism well enough to earn a 4-point
  short-answer.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Starch is made of two kinds of glucose chains. **Amylose** is long and
  straight. **Amylopectin** is branched, like a bush. In water, the straight
  amylose chains coil up into a spring shape — a **helix**.

  The iodine in the reagent isn't just I₂. In solution it's mostly **triiodide**
  — three iodine atoms in a row, I₃⁻ — and longer chains of iodine. Those long
  straight iodine chains slide right **inside** the amylose helix, like a wire
  through a spring.

  Once the iodine is trapped inside, the iodine and the starch **share electrons
  loosely** — chemists call it a **charge-transfer complex**. That shared-electron
  arrangement absorbs almost all the visible light that hits it, so it looks
  **blue-black**.

  Three things to know for full points:
  - It is **not a chemical reaction**. No bonds break, nothing new is made. The
    iodine is just physically **caged inside** the starch helix. The word for one
    molecule caged in another is an **inclusion complex**, or a *clathrate*.
  - **Amylose gives blue-black. Amylopectin and glycogen give red-brown** —
    their branches are too short to hold a long iodine chain.
  - **Heat makes the color disappear** — the helix uncoils when it's warm, the
    iodine falls out — and the color **comes back when it cools** and the helix
    re-forms.
  - **Plain sugars — sucrose, glucose — give no color.** No helix, nothing to
    trap the iodine."

- **[DEMO — coach does any heating:]**
  Make a cornstarch-water paste, add a drop of iodine → deep blue-black. If you
  can heat it (double boiler, microwave in short bursts): the color fades to
  pale/orange. Let it cool → blue-black returns. Narrate: "the helix opened, the
  iodine left; it cooled, the helix closed, the iodine went back in."

- **[ACTIVITY: Answer the 4-Point Question]**
  On paper, each kid writes their own answer to: *"Explain why and what makes
  flour turn black in iodine. (4 points)"* Then read two or three aloud and
  polish them together against this model:
  > Flour contains **starch**. The starch's **amylose** molecules coil into a
  > **helix** (1). **Iodine (as triiodide ions)** slots **inside** the helix (1).
  > The trapped iodine and the starch form a **charge-transfer complex** that
  > absorbs visible light (1), which makes the mixture look **blue-black** (1).
  > It is an **inclusion complex**, not a chemical reaction.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Is the iodine test a chemical reaction? What's the right
  word?" (No — an inclusion complex / clathrate; the iodine is trapped inside the
  amylose helix.)

### 0:44–0:52 (8 min) — Direct Teaching: Why Carbonates Fizz

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "The fizz test. A carbonate or bicarbonate plus an acid releases **carbon
  dioxide gas** — that's the bubbles.

  Word equation for baking soda and vinegar:
  **sodium bicarbonate + acetic acid → sodium acetate + water + carbon dioxide**

  Symbol equation: **NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → NaC₂H₃O₂ + H₂O + CO₂↑**

  The little up-arrow means a gas leaves. Because the gas escapes, the reaction
  keeps going until a reactant runs out — it doesn't reach a stopping balance.

  Chalk — calcium carbonate — fizzes the same way, but it's not soluble, so the
  acid can only reach the surface of each grain. The fizz is slower and you're
  left with cloudy undissolved chalk. That slower, partial fizz is actually a
  *clue* — it says 'carbonate, but not baking soda.'"

- **[ACTIVITY:]** Kids test baking soda vs. chalk with vinegar side by side and
  describe the difference in words for their notes.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "What gas makes the fizz?" (carbon dioxide) "Why is the chalk
  fizz slower?" (it's insoluble — acid only reaches the grain surface)

---

### 0:52–1:02 (10 min) — Reducing Sugars & the Benedict's Test

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "One more test you won't run — the reagent isn't supplied — but it's a
  short-answer question. **Benedict's solution** is blue. When you heat it with a
  **reducing sugar** — glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose — it turns
  **green, then yellow, then brick-red**. Those sugars have a chemical group that
  can 'reduce' the copper in the blue solution, dropping out a red-orange solid.

  **Sucrose — table sugar — and starch are *non-reducing*.** They stay blue,
  unless you first break them down into their smaller sugars.

  So if a question says 'Benedict's turned red,' the answer is 'a reducing sugar
  is present.' If it stayed blue with table sugar, that's expected — sucrose is
  non-reducing."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Benedict's turns brick-red. What does that mean?" (a
  reducing sugar is present) "Does table sugar do that?" (no — sucrose is
  non-reducing)

---

### 1:02–1:18 (16 min) — Main Activity: Predict, Then Test the Iodine Panel

- **Goal:** kids apply the mechanism to predict results, including the tricky
  ones (powdered sugar, baking powder, vitamin C).

- **[ACTIVITY: Predict-Then-Test]**
  Hand out the Predict-Then-Test sheet. Each pair has labeled pinches of:
  cornstarch, flour, granulated sugar, **powdered sugar**, **baking powder**,
  table salt, and a cup of **vitamin C solution**.

  Step 1 — **before testing**, they write a prediction for each: will iodine go
  blue-black, stay orange, or do something else? And *why*, in a few words.

  Step 2 — test each with one drop of iodine. For the vitamin C, add a drop of
  **dilute iodine to the vitamin C solution** and watch.

  Step 3 — explain any surprises.

  Expected results:
  - cornstarch, flour → **blue-black** (starch)
  - granulated sugar, salt → **stays orange** (no starch)
  - **powdered sugar → faint blue** — it has ~3% cornstarch added as anti-caking
  - **baking powder → blue-black** — it contains cornstarch (this is how you tell
    it from plain baking soda!)
  - **vitamin C solution → iodine goes colorless/clear** — vitamin C is a
    **reducing agent**; it chemically converts the iodine to colorless iodide, so
    there's no iodine left to make a color. This one *is* a real reaction, unlike
    the starch test.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Powdered sugar gave a faint blue. Why, if sugar has no
  starch?" (cornstarch is added to powdered sugar to keep it from clumping)
  "Baking powder went black but baking soda doesn't. What's that telling you?"
  (baking powder contains cornstarch — the iodine result separates the two)

### 1:26–1:30 (4 min) — Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Today: the formulas, and the *why*. Iodine + starch = the amylose helix traps
  iodine, forms a charge-transfer complex, absorbs light, looks blue-black — an
  inclusion complex, not a reaction. Carbonate + acid = CO₂ gas. Vitamin C
  decolorizes iodine because it's a reducing agent — that one *is* a reaction."

- **Exit ticket:**
  1. **Give the formula for baking soda, sucrose, and gypsum.** *(NaHCO₃;
     C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁; CaSO₄·2H₂O)*
  2. **In one or two sentences, why does starch turn blue-black with iodine?**
     *(Amylose coils into a helix; iodine slides inside; the trapped iodine forms
     a charge-transfer complex that absorbs light → blue-black; it's an inclusion
     complex, not a reaction.)*
  3. **Baking soda and baking powder both fizz. One test tells them apart —
     which, and how?** *(Iodine — baking powder contains cornstarch so it turns
     blue-black; baking soda alone does not.)*

- **Homework:** Homework 3 — paper track (formula matching + test-mechanism short
  answers) + **Home Lab 3** (starch and carbonate tests at home).

## Coach Notes & Common Kid Mistakes

- **"The iodine test is a chemical reaction."** The single most common wrong
  answer. It's an *inclusion complex* — iodine physically trapped in the helix.
  (The vitamin C decolorizing, by contrast, *is* a reaction — don't let that
  blur the point.)
- **Confusing amylose and amylopectin.** Amylose = straight = blue-black.
  Amylopectin = branched = red-brown. Mnemonic: "amyl**ose** is a r**ose** on a
  straight stem."
- **Writing NaHCO₃ for washing soda.** Washing soda (sodium carbonate) is
  Na₂CO₃ — no hydrogen. Baking soda (bicarbonate) has the H.
- **Forgetting the *n* in the starch formula.** (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ — it's a polymer.
  Same for cellulose/cotton.
- **Expecting vitamin C to turn iodine black because it's a "powder like the
  others."** It has no starch; instead it *removes* the iodine color entirely.
  This trips kids every year.
- **Powdered sugar surprise.** Kids assume "sugar = no iodine" and mark it wrong.
  The added cornstarch gives a real faint blue.
