# Session 4: Qualitative Analysis III — Mixtures & Equations
### Crime Busters Coach Script — [~90 min, dense — do not add to it]

## Before You Start

- **Learning goals:** Each kid can (1) recognize when a sample is a mixture of two
  powders and identify both components, (2) separate a simple mixture by
  solubility (dissolve-and-filter), (3) balance a chemical equation by adjusting
  coefficients, (4) write the balanced equation for baking soda + an acid, and
  (5) follow a worked mole / limiting-reactant calculation and reproduce the
  steps on a similar problem.
- **Year 1 recap built in:** the powder battery and formulas (Sessions 2–3).
- **Materials:**
  - Knowledge Document Chapters 4 (mixtures section) and 7. Read Chapter 7
    carefully.
  - Per pair: a mixture of **table salt + cornstarch** (about 1:1) in a numbered
    cup; a second mystery mixture of your choice (e.g. **baking soda + sand**, or
    **sugar + gypsum**); coffee filters or paper towels, a funnel or just a cup,
    water, iodine, vinegar, spot plate, hand lens.
  - Whiteboard; scratch paper; calculators (with the periodic-table masses handy
    or a printed mini periodic table).
  - Printed **Balancing Relay sheet** and **Mixture Lab sheet** (below).
  - For the finale demo: baking soda, citric acid (or lemon juice), a zip bag, a
    kitchen scale if you have one.
- **Prep:** make the mixtures and number them; write your key; print sheets; test
  the filtration setup once yourself so you know how long it takes.
- **Vocabulary:** mixture · component · filtrate / residue · coefficient ·
  subscript · balanced equation · mole · molar mass · limiting reactant.

> **If you only have 60 minutes:** Do the mixture lab and the balancing relay.
> Cut the mole / limiting-reactant teaching (1:05–1:22) down to a 5-minute
> "here's what a mole is and here's the one formula (moles = mass ÷ molar mass)"
> and move the worked example to Homework 4 with full solution steps provided.

## Segment-by-Segment Script

### 0:00–0:06 (6 min) — Recap & Hook

- **[ASK THE TEAM:]** "Formula for baking soda? Cornstarch? What does the *n*
  mean?" (NaHCO₃; (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ; the unit repeats — it's a polymer)

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Two new things today. One: sometimes a 'sample' is actually two powders mixed,
  and the key wants *both* names. Two: the harder tests give you a chemical
  equation to balance, or even ask you to calculate how much product a reaction
  makes. That sounds like high-school chemistry, and it is — but it's a small,
  learnable piece of it, and it's worth real points."

---

### 0:06–0:20 (14 min) — Direct Teaching: Spotting and Splitting a Mixture

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "How do you know a cup is a mixture and not one powder? Look for **two of
  anything**:
  - Two different crystal shapes or colors under the lens.
  - A **partial** test result — it fizzes a little *and* leaves a residue; iodine
    turns part of the smear black and leaves grains orange; it half-dissolves.

  When a single test gives a split result, ask: **'is there one powder that
  explains all of this?'** If yes, it's that powder. If nothing explains all of
  it, it's a mixture — and each observation belongs to one component.

  To actually separate a mixture, the easiest tool is **solubility**. Add water:
  the soluble part dissolves, the insoluble part doesn't. Pour the liquid through
  a filter — the clear liquid that comes through is the **filtrate**, the stuff
  caught on the paper is the **residue**. Dry each and test them separately.

  Salt plus cornstarch: add water, stir, filter. Salt is in the filtrate — let
  the water evaporate and you get salt crystals back. Cornstarch is the residue
  on the paper. Now you test each one and name both."

- **[ACTIVITY:]** On the board, work through three "which single powder explains
  this, or is it a mixture?" cases:
  - *"Fizzes fully, dissolves fully, pH 9."* → one powder: baking soda.
  - *"Fizzes a little, lots of white residue won't dissolve, iodine negative."*
    → mixture: a carbonate (baking soda) + something insoluble and non-starch
    (sand or gypsum).
  - *"Half dissolves; the dissolved part is neutral; the leftover turns iodine
    black."* → mixture: salt (soluble, neutral) + cornstarch (insoluble, starch).

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "What's the filtrate? What's the residue?" (filtrate = the
  liquid that passes through with the dissolved stuff; residue = what's caught on
  the filter)

---

### 0:20–0:40 (20 min) — Main Activity: Separate & Identify a Mixture

- **[ACTIVITY: Mixture Lab]**
  Each pair gets two numbered mixture cups and the Mixture Lab sheet. For each:
  1. Observe dry — crystal shapes, colors. Predict: mixture or single? Of what?
  2. Add water, stir, **filter**.
  3. Evaporate a few drops of the filtrate on the spot plate (or just observe
     what's dissolved); scrape and dry the residue.
  4. Test the filtrate solid and the residue separately with the battery.
  5. Name **both** components, with formulas.

  Cup 1 (salt + cornstarch): filtrate → salt (NaCl); residue → cornstarch
  ((C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ, iodine positive).
  Cup 2 (your choice — e.g. baking soda + sand): filtrate → baking soda (NaHCO₃,
  fizzes); residue → sand (SiO₂, inert, gritty).

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "You filtered and the residue turned iodine black and the
  dried filtrate was cubic crystals that didn't melt. Name both." (cornstarch +
  table salt)

---

### 0:40–0:55 (15 min) — Direct Teaching: Balancing Equations

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "A chemical equation has to have the **same number of each kind of atom on both
  sides** — atoms aren't created or destroyed. You balance it by putting numbers
  **in front** of the formulas. Those front numbers are **coefficients**. You
  *never* change the little numbers inside a formula — the **subscripts** — those
  define what the substance *is*.

  Method:
  1. Count each element on both sides.
  2. Balance one element at a time by adjusting coefficients. Save hydrogen and
     oxygen for last.
  3. Re-count everything.

  Example — hydrogen gas burning:
  `H₂ + O₂ → H₂O`
  Oxygen: 2 on the left, 1 on the right. Put a 2 in front of the water:
  `H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O`
  Now hydrogen: 2 on the left, 4 on the right. Put a 2 in front of H₂:
  `2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O` — check: H 4=4, O 2=2. Balanced."

  Now the Crime Busters one — **baking soda + citric acid** (this exact equation
  has been on a real test):
  `__ NaHCO₃ + __ H₃C₆H₅O₇ → __ CO₂ + __ H₂O + __ Na₃C₆H₅O₇`
  "The sodium citrate on the right needs **3 sodium**, so we need **3 NaHCO₃** and
  **1 sodium citrate**. That gives us 3 carbons and 3 hydrogens and 9 oxygens
  from the bicarbonate. Work it through:"
  `3 NaHCO₃ + H₃C₆H₅O₇ → 3 CO₂ + 3 H₂O + Na₃C₆H₅O₇`
  Check together: Na 3=3, C 3+6=9 and 3+6=9, H 3+8=11 and 6+5=11, O 9+7=16 and
  6+3+7=16. Balanced.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Can you balance an equation by changing a subscript?" (No —
  only coefficients; changing a subscript changes what the substance is.)

---

### 0:55–1:05 (10 min) — Balancing Relay

- **[ACTIVITY: Balancing Relay]**
  Hand out the Balancing Relay sheet (below) — six equations of increasing
  difficulty. Kids race (solo or in pairs), one equation at a time, checking each
  with you before moving on. Celebrate speed improvement.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** Pick the equation most got wrong and re-work it on the board.

---

### 1:05–1:22 (17 min) — Direct Teaching: A First Look at Moles

- **Goal:** kids can follow (and roughly reproduce) a mass-to-mass calculation.
  This is the hardest content in the whole curriculum; go slowly and it's fine if
  not every kid nails it — the paper homework has full worked steps.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "A **mole** is just a counting word, like 'dozen' — it means 6.02 × 10²³
  particles. Chemists use it because atoms are tiny and you need a huge number of
  them to have any visible amount.

  The **molar mass** of a substance is how many grams one mole weighs. You get it
  by adding up the atomic masses in the formula. From a periodic table: hydrogen
  is about 1, carbon 12, oxygen 16, sodium 23.
  - NaHCO₃ = 23 + 1 + 12 + (3 × 16) = **84 grams per mole**.
  - Citric acid C₆H₈O₇ = (6 × 12) + (8 × 1) + (7 × 16) = 72 + 8 + 112 = **192
    grams per mole**.

  The one formula that does most of the work: **moles = mass ÷ molar mass.**

  Now the hard question type. You mix 15 g of baking soda with 10 g of citric
  acid. How much sodium citrate (Na₃C₆H₅O₇, molar mass 258) do you get?
  - Moles of baking soda = 15 ÷ 84 = **0.179 mol**. Its coefficient is 3, so
    0.179 ÷ 3 = 0.0595.
  - Moles of citric acid = 10 ÷ 192 = **0.052 mol**. Its coefficient is 1, so
    0.052 ÷ 1 = 0.052.
  - 0.052 is smaller → **citric acid is the limiting reactant** — it runs out
    first and decides how much product forms.
  - The equation makes 1 sodium citrate per 1 citric acid, so we get 0.052 mol of
    sodium citrate.
  - Mass = moles × molar mass = 0.052 × 258 = **about 13.4 grams**."

- **[ACTIVITY:]** Give the team a parallel problem with the numbers changed
  (e.g. 21 g baking soda + 12 g citric acid) and walk it through together on the
  board, kids doing each step first.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "What does 'limiting reactant' mean?" (the reactant that runs
  out first — it limits how much product you can make) "How do you find it?"
  (divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient; the smallest number is the
  limiting one)

---

### 1:22–1:28 (6 min) — Finale Demo: Limiting Reactant You Can See

- **[DEMO:]** In a zip bag, put a spoon of baking soda. Add a splash of lemon
  juice or a spoon of citric acid + water, seal fast. The bag puffs with CO₂.
  Do it twice — once with lots of baking soda and a little acid, once the
  reverse. "Whichever one runs out first stops the fizz. The leftover of the
  other one just sits there. That's the limiting reactant, live."

---

### 1:28–1:30 (2 min) — Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket

- **Exit ticket:**
  1. **A sample fizzes a little and leaves white residue that won't dissolve and
     is iodine-negative. One powder or a mixture? Of what?** *(A mixture — a
     carbonate like baking soda plus an insoluble non-starch powder like sand or
     gypsum.)*
  2. **Balance: `__ Al + __ O₂ → __ Al₂O₃`.** *(4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃)*
  3. **What is the molar mass of CO₂?** *(12 + 16 + 16 = 44 g/mol)*

- **Homework:** Homework 4 — paper track (balancing + a fully-worked mole example
  to copy the method from, then one to do) + **Home Lab 4** (separate a
  salt/cornstarch mixture at home by dissolving and filtering).

## Balancing Relay Sheet (print for kids)

Balance each by adjusting coefficients only.

1. `__ H₂ + __ Cl₂ → __ HCl`
2. `__ Mg + __ HCl → __ MgCl₂ + __ H₂`
3. `__ Na + __ Cl₂ → __ NaCl`
4. `__ CH₄ + __ O₂ → __ CO₂ + __ H₂O`
5. `__ Fe + __ O₂ → __ Fe₂O₃`
6. `__ NaHCO₃ + __ HCl → __ NaCl + __ H₂O + __ CO₂`

*(Answers: 1) 1,1,2  2) 1,2,1,1  3) 2,1,2  4) 1,2,1,2  5) 4,3,2  6) 1,1,1,1,1)*

## Coach Notes & Common Kid Mistakes

- **Calling a partial result a "failed test."** A half-fizz with residue is a
  *mixture signal*, not a mistake. Reframe every partial result as information.
- **Changing subscripts to balance.** The classic error. `H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂` "looks
  balanced" but you've made hydrogen peroxide, not water. Coefficients only.
- **Forgetting to re-count after a change.** Balancing one element often unbalances
  another. Always re-count everything at the end.
- **Molar mass arithmetic slips.** Watch the subscripts: CO₂ is 12 + 2(16) = 44,
  not 12 + 16 = 28.
- **Picking the limiting reactant by mass instead of moles.** 15 g vs. 10 g does
  *not* mean baking soda is in excess by "5 g worth" — you must convert to moles
  and divide by coefficients. This is the step kids skip.
- **Overwhelm on the mole material.** It's genuinely hard for grade 5–6. Goal is
  "can follow the worked steps and reproduce them," not fluency. The paper
  homework gives a solved example to pattern-match. Don't let a rough day here
  dent their confidence — most invitational math is Rf, density, and the spatter
  angle, which are all easier.
