# Session 5: Liquids & pH
### Crime Busters Coach Script — [~90 min]

## Before You Start

- **Learning goals:** Each kid can (1) identify the common competition liquids by
  smell, pH, and behavior and give each one's formula, (2) say what pH stands for
  and what a low pH means (more H⁺), (3) explain that the pH scale is
  logarithmic — each unit is a factor of 10, (4) place substances as acid /
  neutral / base and read a red-cabbage indicator, and (5) handle the HCl station
  safely.
- **Year 1 recap built in:** last year's liquid property table (water, vinegar,
  ammonia, hydrogen peroxide, lemon juice, isopropyl alcohol).
- **Materials:**
  - Knowledge Document Chapter 5. Read first.
  - **Red-cabbage indicator** — make a batch (boil chopped red cabbage ~10 min,
    strain; the purple liquid is your indicator). Enough for a class demo plus
    refills of kids' home bottles.
  - Per pair: dropper of cabbage indicator, pH paper (full range), small labeled
    cups of: **white vinegar, lemon juice, water, baking-soda solution, dilute
    dish soap or a very dilute washing-soda solution**. (Household ammonia is
    **coach-only** — strong smell.)
  - **Unknown liquid set** (numbered 1–4): pick from water, vinegar, lemon juice,
    salt water, sugar water, isopropyl alcohol, hydrogen peroxide.
  - For demos: a small piece of raw liver or a chunk of raw potato + hydrogen
    peroxide; a bottle of household ammonia (coach handles); 1 M HCl + goggles +
    gloves + baking soda + a strip of pH paper.
  - Printed **pH Ladder sheet** and **Liquid ID sheet** (below). Whiteboard.
- **Prep:** make the cabbage indicator (do it the night before; refrigerate).
  Portion liquids into labeled cups. Number the unknown set and write your key.
- **Vocabulary:** pH · hydrogen ion (H⁺) · acid · base / alkaline · neutral ·
  indicator · logarithmic scale · neutralization · catalase (in the H₂O₂ demo).

> **If you only have 60 minutes:** Cut the neutralization demo (1:12–1:18) and
> the sugar-water/salt-water distinction in the unknown set (use only 3 unknowns:
> vinegar, water, isopropyl). Keep the pH teaching and the cabbage ladder.

## Segment-by-Segment Script

### 0:00–0:07 (7 min) — Recap & Hook

- **[ASK THE TEAM:]** "From last year — name three liquids that might be at a
  crime scene and one property of each." (water/neutral; vinegar/sour smell;
  ammonia/pungent; hydrogen peroxide/bubbles on a wound; etc.)

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Last year you learned to tell these liquids apart by smell and how they look.
  This year we add two things: the **chemical formula** for each — because
  there's a formula column on the answer sheet — and a real understanding of
  **pH**, because 'what does pH stand for' and 'what does a low pH mean' are
  guaranteed short-answer points, and every liquid ID uses the pH number."

---

### 0:07–0:25 (18 min) — Direct Teaching: The Liquids and Their Formulas

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Here's the reference table. The three columns that matter: **pH, smell, and a
  tell** — one behavior that clinches it."

  Build on the board:

  | Liquid | Formula | pH | Smell | The tell |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Water | H₂O | 7 | none | the control — nothing happens, dries with no residue |
  | Vinegar | CH₃COOH (C₂H₄O₂) | 2–3 | sharp, sour | acid smell + low pH, clear |
  | Lemon juice | ~C₆H₈O₇ | 2–3 | citrus | low pH but **cloudy / yellowish** (vinegar is clear) |
  | Ammonia | NH₃ | 11–12 | sharp, pungent, stings | high pH + that unmistakable smell |
  | Hydrogen peroxide | H₂O₂ | 4–6 | faint | **foams on contact with blood or raw potato** |
  | Isopropyl (rubbing) alcohol | C₃H₈O | ~6–7 | medicinal | **evaporates fast**, flammable |
  | Salt water | NaCl (aq) | 7 | none | neutral, but leaves **cubic crystals** when it dries |
  | Sugar water | C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (aq) | 7 | faint sweet | neutral, dries **sticky**, no crystals |

  "The empirical formula of vinegar — that's a question — is **CH₂O** (you divide
  C₂H₄O₂ by 2)."

- **[DEMO: the hydrogen peroxide tell]**
  Put a piece of raw liver or potato in a cup, pour hydrogen peroxide over it —
  vigorous foaming. "An enzyme called **catalase**, in blood and in potato,
  breaks hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen gas — that's the foam. This is
  also a **presumptive test for blood**. If a mystery liquid foams on a drop of
  blood, it's hydrogen peroxide."

- **[DEMO: ammonia — coach only]**
  Waft a *tiny* amount toward the group from a distance. "That smell is the tell.
  Also the highest pH you'll meet — 11 or 12."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Two clear liquids, both pH 2, both sour. How do you tell
  vinegar from lemon juice?" (lemon juice is cloudy/yellowish; vinegar is clear)
  "A liquid foams when it touches blood. What is it?" (hydrogen peroxide)

---

### 0:25–0:45 (20 min) — Direct Teaching: What pH Actually Is

- **Goal:** kids can answer the exact short-answer questions and understand the
  scale.

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "**pH stands for 'potential of hydrogen'** — some books say 'power of
  hydrogen,' both are accepted. It measures **how many hydrogen ions — H⁺ — are
  in the solution**.

  - **More H⁺ → lower pH → more acidic.**
  - **Fewer H⁺ → higher pH → more basic** (basic also means 'alkaline').
  - **pH 7 is neutral** — pure water.
  - The usual scale runs **0 to 14**.

  The exact question you'll see: *'If a liquid has a small pH value, what does it
  have a higher concentration of?'* Answer: **hydrogen ions, H⁺.**

  Now the part people miss. The scale is **logarithmic** — each whole number is a
  factor of **ten**. pH 3 has **ten times** more H⁺ than pH 4. pH 2 has **a
  hundred times** more than pH 4. So the difference between vinegar at pH 2 and
  water at pH 7 isn't '5 units,' it's **a hundred thousand times** more hydrogen
  ions.

  There's a formula for the older kids: **pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]**. You don't need to
  compute it, but if you see it, that's what it is."

  **Acids vs. bases — the level tested:**
  - An **acid** gives off H⁺. Tastes sour, turns litmus red, turns cabbage
    indicator pink/red, fizzes with carbonates, reacts with active metals to make
    hydrogen gas.
  - A **base** takes up H⁺ (gives off OH⁻). Tastes bitter, feels slippery, turns
    litmus blue, turns cabbage indicator green/yellow.
  - **Acid + base → salt + water.** That's **neutralization**. Example:
    `HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O`.

- **[ACTIVITY: The Number Line]**
  Draw a 0–14 line on the board. Have kids come up and place cards: lemon juice
  (2), vinegar (2.5), black coffee (5), pure water (7), baking soda solution (9),
  hand soap (10), household ammonia (11), bleach (13). Then ask: "how many times
  more acidic is the lemon juice than the coffee?" (3 units → 10 × 10 × 10 =
  1000×)

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "What does pH stand for?" (potential of hydrogen) "Low pH
  means more of what?" (H⁺ / hydrogen ions) "pH 4 vs pH 6 — how many times more
  acidic?" (100×)

---

### 0:45–1:00 (15 min) — Activity: The Cabbage-Indicator pH Ladder

- **[ACTIVITY: pH Ladder]**
  Each pair sets up a row of cups and adds a few drops of cabbage indicator to
  each, then records the color and estimates the pH from the color chart:

  | Color | Approx. pH |
  |---|---|
  | red | 2 |
  | pink | 4 |
  | purple | 7 |
  | blue-purple | 8 |
  | blue-green | 9–10 |
  | green | 11 |
  | yellow | 12+ |

  Test: vinegar, lemon juice, water, baking-soda solution, dilute washing soda or
  soap. (Coach adds a drop of ammonia to one cup at the front so they see the
  green/yellow end.)

  Then: **make a rainbow.** Start with a cup of indicator, add vinegar
  drop-by-drop to go red, then baking soda solution to walk it back up through
  purple to blue to green. This is neutralization happening in front of them.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Your cabbage juice turned bright pink in a mystery liquid.
  Acid or base? Roughly what pH?" (acid; pH ~3–4)

---

### 1:00–1:12 (12 min) — Main Activity: Identify the Unknown Liquids

- **[ACTIVITY: Liquid ID]**
  Each pair gets numbered unknowns 1–4 and the Liquid ID sheet. For each:
  smell (waft!), pH (paper + cabbage), appearance, one behavior test (does it
  evaporate fast? does it leave residue? does it foam on the bit of potato you
  have?). Name it, give the formula.

  Suggested set: (1) water, (2) vinegar, (3) isopropyl alcohol, (4) salt water —
  the salt-vs-sugar water or the peroxide can be added for a stronger group.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Unknown 4 was pH 7, no smell, and left cubic crystals when a
  drop dried. What is it and what's the formula?" (salt water — NaCl in water)

---

### 1:12–1:20 (8 min) — Coach Demo + Safety: The HCl Station

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "At competition you get **1 molar hydrochloric acid**. It's a real strong acid
  — much stronger than anything in your home kit. Here's how the station runs:
  goggles down, gloves on, work over the tray, use **drops**, cap the bottle
  between every use, and if anything splashes you, flood it with water and tell
  the supervisor immediately. You'll use it for the carbonate powders and for the
  metals next week."

  **[DEMO:]** With full protection, add a few drops of 1 M HCl to baking soda in
  the tray (fast fizz) and touch a strip of pH paper to the acid (bright red,
  pH ~0–1). "That's what a strong acid looks like on paper — right at the bottom
  of the scale."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Where do you use HCl?" (only here, with the coach, in
  goggles) "Home stand-in for acid tests?" (vinegar)

---

### 1:20–1:30 (10 min) — Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "pH is 'potential of hydrogen' — it measures H⁺ concentration. Low pH = lots of
  H⁺ = acidic. The scale is logarithmic, each step is 10×. Acid + base →
  salt + water. And the liquid tells: lemon is the cloudy sour one, peroxide
  foams on blood, isopropyl evaporates fast, ammonia you smell from across the
  room."

- **Exit ticket:**
  1. **What does pH stand for, and what does a low pH mean a solution has more
     of?** *(Potential (or power) of hydrogen; more hydrogen ions, H⁺.)*
  2. **Give the formula for water, vinegar, and hydrogen peroxide.** *(H₂O;
     CH₃COOH or C₂H₄O₂; H₂O₂)*
  3. **A clear, sour-smelling liquid reads pH 2.5 on the paper and is not cloudy.
     What is it — and what would tell you it was lemon juice instead?**
     *(Vinegar; lemon juice would be cloudy/yellowish.)*

- **Homework:** Homework 5 — paper track (liquid ID from clues + the pH scale) +
  **Home Lab 5** (build a cabbage-indicator pH ladder at home).

## Coach Notes & Common Kid Mistakes

- **"pH stands for percentage of hydrogen"** or "power of health" — no. Potential
  (or power) of Hydrogen.
- **Thinking the scale is linear.** pH 4 to pH 2 is not "twice as acidic," it's
  100×. Drill this — it's a favorite question.
- **Sniffing directly.** Every year someone puts their nose to the ammonia. Waft
  only, and keep ammonia at the front as a coach demo.
- **Confusing vinegar and lemon juice.** Both sour, both pH ~2. The difference is
  appearance (lemon = cloudy/yellow) and sometimes the citrus smell.
- **Forgetting hydrogen peroxide's tell.** They know "it bubbles on a cut" but
  don't connect that the bubbling *is* the identification test at a scene.
- **Isopropyl vs. water confusion.** Both clear and thin. Isopropyl evaporates
  fast off the back of a hand and smells medicinal; water doesn't.
- **Leaving goggles off for the cabbage-indicator work** because "it's just
  cabbage juice." Goggles on for the whole lab — the vinegar and any washing-soda
  solution still deserve eye protection, and it builds the habit.
