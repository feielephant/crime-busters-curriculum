# Session 8: Fibers & Chromatography
### Crime Busters Coach Script — [~90 min]

## Before You Start

- **Learning goals:** Each kid can (1) run the fiber burn-test flow chart to
  classify a fiber as animal / vegetable / synthetic from described results,
  (2) name specific fibers in each class and say synthetics come from petroleum,
  (3) explain moisture-wicking as capillary action in synthetic fabric, (4) run a
  paper chromatography strip correctly and calculate Rf, (5) say what the
  stationary and mobile phases are and what Rf physically means, and (6) match a
  crime-scene ink to a suspect's pen by comparing spot patterns and Rf values.
- **Year 1 recap built in:** fiber classes, transfer types, basic chromatography
  and Rf.
- **Materials:**
  - Knowledge Document Chapters 9 and 10. Read first.
  - **Burn-test demo** (coach only, outdoors or by a window with water nearby):
    small snippets of wool, cotton, polyester, and a nylon (labeled). Tongs,
    a lighter, a metal dish, a cup of water.
  - Per pair: coffee-filter or chromatography paper strips, pencils, rulers,
    clear cups, water (and optionally a little salt water or rubbing alcohol as
    alternate solvents), 3–4 **water-soluble markers** of different brands/colors
    (black markers separate beautifully), and one designated "crime-scene note"
    written with one of them.
  - Printed **Burn-Test Flow Chart**, **Chromatography sheet** (below). Whiteboard.
- **Prep:** cut paper strips; label the burn samples; write the "crime-scene
  note" with a specific marker and keep track of which; test that your markers
  actually separate on your paper with your water.
- **Vocabulary:** animal / protein fiber · vegetable / cellulose fiber ·
  synthetic fiber · rayon (regenerated cellulose) · capillary action ·
  stationary phase · mobile phase · solvent front · retention factor (Rf) ·
  Mikhail Tsvet.

> **If you only have 60 minutes:** Do the burn-test demo and flow-chart practice
> quickly (10 min total) and spend the rest on chromatography — running strips
> and computing Rf is the higher-yield skill and needs the hands-on time.

## Segment-by-Segment Script

### 0:00–0:06 (6 min) — Recap & Hook

- **[ASK THE TEAM:]** "Three classes of fiber?" (animal, vegetable, synthetic)
  "What's Rf?" (distance the component moved ÷ distance the solvent moved) "What's
  direct vs. secondary transfer?" (source→person directly vs. source→person→person)

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Two topics that go together because they often share a station — fibers and
  chromatography. New this year: the **burn test as a decision flow chart** you
  can run from *described* results, naming **specific** fibers and where
  synthetics come from, the **capillary action** behind moisture-wicking clothes,
  and the chromatography **vocabulary** a key wants plus matching an ink to a
  pen."

---

### 0:06–0:22 (16 min) — Direct Teaching + Demo: The Fiber Burn Test

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "You will not burn fibers at competition — but they may **give you the burn
  results** and ask you to classify. So learn it as a flow chart.

  | Class | Near the flame | In the flame | Smell | What's left |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | **Animal / protein** (wool, silk) | curls away | burns slowly, sputters, **self-extinguishes** | **burning hair / feathers** | dark, brittle, crushable bead |
  | **Vegetable / cellulose** (cotton, linen) | ignites readily | burns steadily, afterglow | **burning paper / leaves** | light gray feathery ash, **no bead** |
  | **Synthetic** (nylon, polyester, acrylic) | **shrinks / melts away** from flame | melts, drips, may flame | acrid / chemical / sweet plastic | **hard shiny plastic bead** |
  | **Rayon** (regenerated cellulose) | ignites readily | burns **fast**, little melting | burning paper | little ash |

  The flow chart:
  ```
  Smells like burning HAIR? ── yes ──► ANIMAL
                            ── no ──► Melts/shrinks away, leaves a hard bead?
                                       ── yes ──► SYNTHETIC
                                       ── no ──► smells like PAPER, gray ash ──► VEGETABLE
  ```

  **Where synthetics come from:** most are polymers made from **petroleum** (and
  historically coal). The exceptions are **rayon and acetate**, which are made by
  chemically **regenerating cellulose from wood pulp** — so they're called
  *semi-synthetic*.

  Specific fibers to know:
  - Animal: **wool** (sheep), silk (silkworm), cashmere/mohair (goat), angora
    (rabbit).
  - Vegetable: **cotton** (the most common), linen (flax), hemp, jute.
  - Synthetic: **polyester, nylon, acrylic, olefin/polypropylene, spandex**."

- **[DEMO — coach only, ventilated, water at hand:]**
  Burn a small snippet of each labeled fiber with tongs over the metal dish. Have
  kids call out the class from the flow chart as you go, and note the smell and
  residue. Drop each into water when done.

- **[ACTIVITY: Flow-Chart Practice]**
  Give the team 5 described burn results (below, in the sheet) and have them
  classify each and name a likely specific fiber.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "A fiber shrinks away from the flame, melts into a hard bead,
  and smells like chemicals. Class? Name one fiber it could be." (synthetic;
  polyester or nylon) "Smells like burning hair and self-puts-out?" (animal —
  wool or silk)

### 0:22–0:30 (8 min) — Direct Teaching: Moisture Wicking

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "A real 3-point question: *'What fiber is advertised to wick moisture, and how
  does it work?'*

  Answer: **synthetic** (polyester, polypropylene). The trick is that the fibers
  themselves **don't absorb** water. Instead the tiny channels **between** the
  fibers pull sweat outward by **capillary action** — the same thing that pulls
  water up a paper towel or up a chromatography strip. The sweat spreads across
  the outer surface where it evaporates fast, so the fabric feels dry.

  Cotton does the opposite — it soaks water *into* each fiber and stays wet and
  heavy. That's why athletic wear is synthetic, not cotton."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Cotton or polyester for a running shirt, and why?"
  (polyester — it wicks sweat outward by capillary action between the fibers and
  dries fast; cotton absorbs and stays wet)

---

### 0:30–0:45 (15 min) — Direct Teaching: Chromatography, Properly

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Chromatography separates a mixture — like the different dyes in one marker —
  by how strongly each part sticks to the paper versus travels with the liquid.

  - **Stationary phase = the paper** (cellulose, with water bound to it). It
    stays put.
  - **Mobile phase = the solvent** that climbs the paper (water, salt water,
    alcohol).
  - A component that's **more soluble in the solvent / less attracted to the
    paper** travels **farther**. One that grips the paper stays near the bottom.

  **Rf — the retention factor** (also called retardation factor):
  **Rf = distance the component moved ÷ distance the solvent front moved.**
  - Always between **0 and 1** — a component can't outrun the solvent carrying it.
  - Stuck on the start line → Rf = 0. Moves with the front → Rf ≈ 1.
  - Under the **same conditions** (same paper, same solvent, same temperature) an
    Rf is **reproducible** — that's what makes it useful for matching.

  History question: the word **chromatography** is Greek for 'color writing.'
  Invented by **Mikhail Tsvet**, a Russian botanist, around 1900, separating
  plant pigments.

  Procedure that scores:
  1. **Pencil** a start line ~1.5 cm from the bottom (never pen — the pen ink
     would run!).
  2. Small concentrated spot on the line; dry it; re-spot for a darker spot.
  3. Solvent level **below** the start line — the spot must not be underwater.
  4. Let the solvent climb to about **¾** of the strip.
  5. Take it out, **immediately** mark the solvent front in pencil (it
     evaporates).
  6. Mark the center of each separated color. Measure from the start line to the
     front and to each color."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Why pencil and not pen for the start line?" (pen ink would
  dissolve and run up the paper) "Rf range?" (0 to 1)

---

### 0:45–1:10 (25 min) — Main Activity: Match the Ink to the Pen

- **[ACTIVITY: Ink Chromatography]**
  Set the scene: "A note was left at the crime scene. Four suspects each carry a
  marker. Which one wrote the note?"

  Each pair:
  1. Runs a strip for the **crime-scene note ink** and a strip for each
     **suspect marker** (or share strips across the team — one marker per kid).
  2. Same solvent, same paper, same run for all — controls matter.
  3. Measures and computes **Rf for each distinct spot** on every strip.
  4. Compares: which suspect's strip has the **same number of spots at the same
     Rf values** as the crime-scene strip?
  5. Fills the **Chromatography sheet** and writes a one-sentence conclusion.

  Let dried strips be taped into their notebooks — real tests ask you to attach
  your chromatograms.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Two suspect strips both have a blue and a yellow spot. One
  matches the note's Rf values, one doesn't. Which suspect wrote the note?" (the
  one whose Rf values match — same spots at same positions)

## Burn-Test Flow Chart Practice (print for kids)

Classify each and name a likely specific fiber.

1. Curls away from the flame, burns slowly with sputtering, self-extinguishes,
   smells like singed hair, leaves a black crushable bead. → ______________
2. Catches quickly, burns evenly with an afterglow, smells like burning paper,
   leaves light gray ash. → ______________
3. Pulls back and melts before the flame touches it, drips, hardens into a shiny
   bead, sharp chemical smell. → ______________
4. Burns very fast with almost no melting, smells like paper, very little ash.
   → ______________ *(rayon — regenerated cellulose)*
5. Burns slowly, self-extinguishes, smells like burning hair — but it's smooth
   and shiny, not scaly. → ______________ *(animal — silk)*

## Coach Notes & Common Kid Mistakes

- **Pen on the start line.** The single most common chromatography mistake — the
  start line and labels must be **pencil**.
- **Spot underwater.** If the spot is below the solvent level it just dissolves
  into the reservoir. Solvent goes *below* the line.
- **Not marking the solvent front immediately.** It evaporates in seconds and
  then you can't compute Rf. Mark it the instant the strip comes out.
- **Comparing colors instead of Rf.** Two blacks can separate into different dye
  sets. Match by **number of spots and their Rf values**, not by "they're both
  black."
- **Rf greater than 1.** Impossible — means they measured the component past the
  solvent front, or measured from the wrong line. Rf is always 0–1.
- **Burning fibers at home.** Never. The burn test is a coach demo only. Kids
  classify from *described* results on paper.
- **"All synthetics are man-made from nothing natural."** Rayon and acetate come
  from wood-pulp cellulose — that's why they're "regenerated" / semi-synthetic.
