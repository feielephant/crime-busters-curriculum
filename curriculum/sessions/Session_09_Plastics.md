# Session 9: Plastics
### Crime Busters Coach Script — [~90 min]

## Before You Start

- **Learning goals:** Each kid can (1) identify the rules plastics (PETE, HDPE,
  PVC, LDPE, PP, PS, PMMA, PC) using a density-column ladder plus provided burn
  results, (2) explain thermoplastic vs. thermoset and state that all rules
  plastics are thermoplastic, (3) map resin codes 1–7 to plastic names and common
  uses, (4) distinguish LDPE from HDPE, and (5) name the first fully synthetic
  plastic and its inventor and year.
- **Year 1 recap built in:** the plastics list, the water float test, burn
  behaviors.
- **Materials:**
  - Knowledge Document Chapter 11. Read first.
  - **Known plastic samples**, labeled, small pieces that sink/float cleanly:
    PETE (soda bottle wall), HDPE (milk jug), LDPE (grocery bag — fold into a
    pellet), PP (bottle cap or yogurt tub), PS (clear cutlery or a CD case
    fragment), PMMA (acrylic offcut), PVC (a bit of pipe or an old credit card),
    and PC if you can get it (water-cooler jug, safety-glasses lens).
  - Per pair: clear cups/jars and labeled solvents for the **density ladder**:
    70% isopropyl (~0.79), vegetable oil (~0.92), water (1.00), saturated salt
    water (~1.20), light corn syrup (~1.33). Tweezers, paper towels.
  - Resin-code scavenger: a bin of clean household plastic items with visible
    recycling numbers.
  - Printed **Density Ladder sheet**, **Plastic ID sheet**, **Resin Code sheet**
    (below). Whiteboard.
- **Prep:** cut and label known samples; mix the salt water to saturation; label
  every solvent with its density; gather scavenger items; print sheets. **Test
  each known plastic in each solvent yourself** and write the float/sink ladder —
  real samples vary.
- **Vocabulary:** polymer · monomer · resin code · density column · thermoplastic
  · thermoset · cross-linked · cured · Bakelite · polyolefin.

> **If you only have 60 minutes:** Use a 3-liquid ladder (isopropyl, water,
> saturated salt water) and 5 known plastics. Cut the resin-code scavenger and
> assign it as part of Homework 9.

## Segment-by-Segment Script

### 0:00–0:06 (6 min) — Recap & Hook

- **[ASK THE TEAM:]** "Which plastics float in water?" (PP, LDPE, HDPE — the
  polyethylenes and polypropylene) "What does the recycling number on a bottle
  tell you?" (which resin/plastic it is)

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Last year: the list, and float-or-sink in water. This year: water alone only
  splits the group in half — we'll build a **density ladder** of several liquids
  to pin down each one; you'll learn **thermoplastic vs. thermoset** and why it
  matters; the **resin codes and their uses**; and a couple of history points
  that show up as tiebreakers."

---

### 0:06–0:22 (16 min) — Direct Teaching: The Plastics & the Density Ladder

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Here's the reference. The density number is the key — burn behavior is a
  backup they *tell* you, not something you test."

  | Code | Plastic | Density (g/cm³) | Water | Uses |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | **PETE** | 1.38–1.39 | sinks | soda bottles, polyester fiber |
  | 2 | **HDPE** | 0.94–0.97 | floats | milk jugs, detergent bottles, piping |
  | 3 | **PVC** | ~1.38 (1.16–1.45) | sinks | pipe, siding, "vinyl," wire insulation |
  | 4 | **LDPE** | 0.92–0.94 | floats | grocery bags, squeeze bottles, film |
  | 5 | **PP** | 0.90–0.92 | floats (best) | yogurt tubs, caps, straws, rope |
  | 6 | **PS** | 1.04–1.07 | sinks | cutlery, CD cases, foam cups (expanded) |
  | 7 | **PMMA** (acrylic) | 1.17–1.20 | sinks | lenses, aquariums, signs |
  | 7 | **PC** (polycarbonate) | 1.20–1.22 | sinks | bulletproof glass, big water jugs, DVDs |

  "Memory hook for the water test: **only the polyolefins float** — PP, LDPE,
  HDPE. Everything else sinks.

  **The density ladder.** A plastic **floats** in any liquid **denser** than it
  and **sinks** in any liquid **less dense** than it. So put a sample through a
  series of liquids and find where it flips from float to sink — that brackets its
  density.

  | Liquid | Density |
  |---|---|
  | 70% isopropyl alcohol | ~0.79 |
  | vegetable oil | ~0.92 |
  | water | 1.00 |
  | saturated salt water | ~1.20 |
  | light corn syrup | ~1.33 |

  Example: a sample **sinks** in isopropyl (0.79) but **floats** in vegetable oil
  (0.92) → density between 0.79 and 0.92 → that's **PP** (0.90–0.92). Another:
  floats in water, sinks in isopropyl, sinks in oil → between 0.92 and 1.00 →
  LDPE. Sinks in water, floats in saturated salt water → between 1.00 and 1.20 →
  PS. Sinks in saturated salt water, floats in corn syrup → between 1.20 and 1.33
  → PMMA, PC, PETE, or PVC (use the provided burn result to finish)."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "A plastic floats in vegetable oil. What does that tell you
  about its density?" (less than ~0.92 → it's PP)

---

### 0:22–0:42 (20 min) — Main Activity: Build the Ladder, ID the Unknowns

- **[ACTIVITY: Density Ladder]**
  Each pair sets up the 5 labeled solvent cups low-to-high density. First they
  **calibrate**: drop in 2–3 *known* plastics and confirm the float/sink pattern
  matches the table (adjust for reality — write the class's agreed ladder on the
  board).

  Then **unknowns**: numbered plastic pieces. For each, record float (F) / sink
  (S) in every liquid, read off the density bracket, and name it — using a
  **provided burn result** card for any that land in the crowded high-density
  zone:
  - "dense black sooty smoke, styrene smell" → **PS**
  - "self-extinguishes, acrid HCl smell" → **PVC**
  - "bright crackling flame, fruity smell" → **PMMA**
  - "melts with black smoke, partly self-extinguishing, from a bottle" → **PETE**
  - "very hard and clear, faint sweet smell, 'bulletproof'" → **PC**

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Your sample sinks in everything up to saturated salt water,
  floats in corn syrup, and the burn card says 'dense black soot, styrene smell.'
  Wait — PS floats in salt water. Re-check." (Teaching moment: PS density
  1.04–1.07 floats in saturated salt water at 1.20. If it sank in salt water it's
  denser — PMMA/PC/PVC/PETE — and 'styrene smell' would be inconsistent. Cross-check
  density and burn; if they conflict, re-measure.)

---

### 0:42–0:55 (13 min) — Direct Teaching: Thermoplastic vs. Thermoset & History

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "**Thermoplastic vs. thermoset** — this is a 4-point question.

  | | Thermoplastic | Thermoset |
  |---|---|---|
  | Chains | long, **not** linked together | **cross-linked** into one rigid network |
  | Heat | **softens, re-melts** — remold it again and again | **doesn't melt** — it chars and breaks down |
  | Curing | none | **cured once** (heat, UV, or a catalyst) — permanent |
  | Recycling | meltable → mechanically recyclable | not meltable → not mechanically recyclable |
  | Examples | **all the rules plastics** | Bakelite, epoxy, polyurethane, vulcanized rubber |

  Fill-in-the-blank you'll see: *'All plastics in the rules occur in
  thermo\_\_\_\_\_ form'* → **thermoplastic**.

  **History / tiebreakers:**
  - The **first fully synthetic plastic** was **Bakelite**, made by **Leo
    Baekeland** in **1907** (patented 1909). Earlier plastics like celluloid were
    based on modified cellulose, so they don't count as 'fully synthetic.'
    Bakelite is a **thermoset**.
  - Only about **9%** of all plastic ever made has been recycled.
  - Resin codes 1–7 are **identification** codes — 1 and 2 are widely recycled,
    the rest much less.

  **LDPE vs. HDPE** — same chemistry, different packing. **HDPE** is stiffer,
  cloudier, denser. **LDPE** is softer, clearer, more flexible. Squeeze test:
  crinkly and stiff = HDPE; soft and stretchy = LDPE."

- **[ACTIVITY: Resin-Code Scavenger]**
  Kids sort the bin of household plastics by their stamped recycling number and
  fill the Resin Code sheet — number, plastic name, item, one other use.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Thermoset or thermoplastic: a plastic fork you can't melt
  down and remold?" (that would be thermoset — but note the rules PS fork *is*
  thermoplastic; the question is about the property) "First fully synthetic
  plastic, inventor, year?" (Bakelite, Leo Baekeland, 1907)

---

### 0:55–1:20 (25 min) — Consolidation + Combined Practice

- **[ACTIVITY:]** Note-sheet **plastics block**: the density table with resin
  codes and uses, the water-float memory hook, the density-ladder logic, the
  thermoplastic/thermoset table, Bakelite facts, LDPE vs. HDPE.

- **[ACTIVITY: Two Quick Scenario Cards]** Each gives a density-ladder result, a
  burn result, and a suspect list; kids name the plastic and the suspect and
  write the one-line justification.

---

### 1:20–1:30 (10 min) — Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket

- **Exit ticket:**
  1. **Name the three plastics that float in water.** *(PP, LDPE, HDPE)*
  2. **A sample sinks in isopropyl alcohol (0.79) but floats in vegetable oil
     (0.92). Which plastic?** *(PP)*
  3. **What is the difference between a thermoplastic and a thermoset?**
     *(Thermoplastic softens and can be re-melted and remolded; thermoset is
     cross-linked and cured once — it chars instead of melting and can't be
     remolded.)*

- **Homework:** Homework 9 — paper track (resin codes + density-ladder reasoning)
  + **Home Lab 9** (build a simple density column at home with water, oil,
  isopropyl, and salt water, and test household plastic bits).

## Coach Notes & Common Kid Mistakes

- **Trapped air bubbles** on a sample make it "float" falsely. Push it under and
  tap it, or wet it first with the solvent.
- **Foam/expanded polystyrene** floats on everything because of the air, not the
  polymer. Use a solid PS piece (cutlery, CD case) for density work.
- **Not calibrating the ladder.** Real solvent densities and real plastic samples
  drift. Always drop in knowns first and write the class's actual ladder.
- **Confusing "thermoplastic" with "soft."** It's about *re-meltability*, not
  hardness. Acrylic is hard and still thermoplastic.
- **Saying celluloid was the first synthetic plastic.** Celluloid is
  modified natural cellulose. **Bakelite** (1907) is the first *fully* synthetic.
- **Guessing PVC by density alone** — its range overlaps PETE. Use the burn
  result (self-extinguishing, acrid) to separate them.
