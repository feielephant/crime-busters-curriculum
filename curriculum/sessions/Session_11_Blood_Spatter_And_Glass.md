# Session 11: Blood Spatter & Glass
### Crime Busters Coach Script — [~90 min]

## Before You Start

- **Learning goals:** Each kid can (1) tell drop direction from stain shape (the
  tail points the way it was going), (2) calculate the angle of impact with
  `arcsin(width ÷ length)`, (3) estimate the area of convergence from several
  stains, (4) classify spatter as low / medium / high velocity by mechanism and
  drop size, (5) tell radial from concentric glass cracks and use the 3R rule,
  and (6) determine bullet direction from a cone fracture and sequence multiple
  impacts.
- **Year 1 recap built in:** the basic idea of spatter velocity, angle, and drop
  height vs. diameter.
- **Materials:**
  - Knowledge Document Chapters 14 and 15. Read first.
  - Calculators **with `sin⁻¹` (arcsin)** — check every kid's calculator does
    this before the session.
  - Printed **spatter-stain diagrams** with rulers (ellipses of known width and
    length at various angles — you draw these, or print a free BPA worksheet;
    don't put copyrighted images in the repo), a **convergence diagram** (several
    elliptical stains on a "wall" with room to draw axis lines), string or a
    straightedge.
  - Optional hands-on: a dropper of water + red food coloring, a taped-down sheet
    of paper on a clipboard you can tilt to set angles (30°, 45°, 60°, 90°), a
    protractor — kids drop "blood" at set angles and measure the stains.
  - **Glass fracture** diagrams: a pane with radial + concentric cracks labeled;
    a two-hole pane for sequencing; a cross-section showing a cone fracture.
  - Printed **Spatter Angle sheet**, **Glass Fracture sheet** (below). Whiteboard.
- **Prep:** draw/print the stain and glass diagrams with real measurements; work
  every calculation yourself; verify calculators.
- **Vocabulary:** passive / low / medium / high velocity · cast-off · arterial
  spurt · satellite spatter · spine · parent drop · area of convergence · area of
  origin · angle of impact · radial crack · concentric crack · 3R rule · cone
  (Hertzian) fracture · void.

> **If you only have 60 minutes:** Cut the hands-on water-drop activity and use
> only the printed stain diagrams. Keep both the angle calculation and the glass
> fracture teaching — both are pure test points.

## Segment-by-Segment Script

### 0:00–0:06 (6 min) — Recap & Hook

- **[ASK THE TEAM:]** "From last year — what are the rough velocity categories of
  blood spatter?" (low/passive, medium, high) "Does a bigger drop mean higher or
  lower energy?" (lower — big drops are gravity/passive; a fine mist is
  high-energy)

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Last year the rules said 'no calculations' for spatter. That's changed — recent
  tests ask you to **calculate the angle of impact**. So today you'll learn that
  formula and practice it, plus finding where the blood came from. Then glass —
  which cracks form first, which way a bullet went, and which impact happened
  first."

---

### 0:06–0:22 (16 min) — Direct Teaching: Spatter Shape, Direction, Velocity

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "**Shape tells direction.**
  - A drop falling **straight down** onto a flat surface makes a **round** stain.
  - A drop hitting at an **angle** makes an **elongated, tear-drop** stain with a
    pointed **tail**. **The tail points in the direction the drop was
    traveling.**
  - **Satellite spatter** (little droplets around a big one) and **spines** (the
    spiky edges) also point outward in the direction of motion.

  **Velocity categories** — defined by the *mechanism*, not just speed:

  | Category | Drop size | Cause |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Low / passive** | large, > 4 mm — drips, pools, flows | gravity only — dripping wound; ~5 ft/s or less |
  | **Medium** | ~1–4 mm | **blunt force**, stabbing, some cast-off; ~5–25 ft/s |
  | **High** | fine **mist**, < 1 mm | **gunshot**, explosion, machinery; > 100 ft/s |

  Also: **cast-off** (a line of stains flung off a swinging weapon), **arterial
  spurt** (big, rhythmic, bright red), **transfer** (an object or hand pressed
  into wet blood), and a **void** — a blank gap in a spatter pattern where a
  person or object blocked the blood, telling you something (or someone) was
  there."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "A stain is a long thin teardrop with the point aimed at the
  door. Which way was the blood going?" (toward the door) "Fine mist of tiny
  stains — what caused it?" (high velocity — gunshot or similar)

---

### 0:22–0:40 (18 min) — Direct Teaching + Practice: The Angle of Impact

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "When a drop hits at an angle, the stain stretches out. The **more slanted** the
  hit, the **longer and thinner** the stain. There's a clean relationship:

  **sin(angle of impact) = width of the stain ÷ length of the stain**

  So to get the angle:

  **angle = arcsin(width ÷ length)** — on your calculator that's the `sin⁻¹`
  button.

  - A **round** stain: width ≈ length, ratio ≈ 1, arcsin(1) = **90°** — it fell
    straight down / hit head-on.
  - A long thin stain: small ratio → small angle — a **glancing** hit.

  Example: a stain **6 mm wide and 12 mm long**. Ratio = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5.
  arcsin(0.5) = **30°**.

  Another: 5 mm wide, 5.8 mm long. 5 ÷ 5.8 = 0.862. arcsin(0.862) ≈ **59.5°**."

- **[ACTIVITY: Spatter Angle sheet]**
  Kids measure width and length of 5 printed stains and compute the angle for
  each. Then, if doing the hands-on version: tilt the clipboard to a set angle,
  drop "blood," measure the resulting stain, compute the angle, and compare to the
  protractor-set angle.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Stain 3 mm wide, 6 mm long — angle?" (arcsin 0.5 = 30°)
  "What angle gives a perfectly round stain?" (90°)

---

### 0:40–0:52 (12 min) — Area of Convergence & Origin

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Several stains from one event point back to where the blood came from.

  **Area of convergence** — the 2-D spot on the surface. Draw a straight line
  through the **long axis** of each elongated stain, back toward the fat end.
  Where the lines cross is the convergence — directly below or beside the source.

  **Area of origin** — the actual 3-D point in space, including **height**. You
  get that by also using the **impact angles**: the steeper the angle, the higher
  the source. Analysts used to run actual strings from each stain at its angle;
  now it's done with software. You won't compute origin height, but know that
  convergence + angles → origin."

- **[ACTIVITY: Convergence Diagram]**
  On the printed diagram, kids draw the long-axis line through each stain and mark
  where the lines meet. One sentence: "the blood source was located near [mark],
  about [low/mid/high] based on the steep/shallow angles."

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "You've found the area of convergence. What extra information
  do you need to find the area of *origin*?" (the impact angles of the stains —
  they give the height)

---

### 0:52–1:10 (18 min) — Direct Teaching + Practice: Glass Fracture

- **COACH SCRIPT:**
  "Two kinds of cracks around an impact in glass:
  - **Radial cracks** shoot **outward** from the hole like spokes. They form
    **first**, and they begin on the side **opposite** the force (that side gets
    stretched).
  - **Concentric cracks** make **circles** around the hole, connecting the
    radials. They form **second**, on the **same** side as the force.

  **The 3R rule:** **R**adial cracks — the stress ridges on the broken edge meet
  the glass surface at a **R**ight angle on the **R**everse side — the side the
  force came *from*. Reading those tiny curved lines on a crack edge tells you
  which side was hit.

  **Cone (Hertzian) fracture:** a fast projectile — BB, bullet — punches a
  **cone-shaped** hole, **narrow on the entry side, wide on the exit side**. So
  the **wide opening faces the way the projectile was going** — that gives you
  direction of fire.

  **Sequencing two impacts:** a crack **stops** when it hits an existing crack.
  So if the radial cracks from hole B **end** where they meet cracks from hole A,
  then **A happened first**.

  **Speed vs. cracking:** a **high-speed** projectile makes a clean hole with
  **little** radial cracking (glass has no time to flex). A **slow** impact — a
  thrown rock, a fist — makes **lots** of radial and concentric cracking. Heat or
  slow pressure makes wavy, non-radial cracks."

- **[ACTIVITY: Glass Fracture sheet]**
  Kids label radial vs. concentric on a diagram; given a cone cross-section, mark
  entry and exit and the direction of fire; given a two-hole pane, state which
  impact was first and why; given "clean hole, minimal cracks" vs. "shattered
  with many radials," say which was high vs. low velocity.

- **[CHECKPOINT:]** "Which cracks form first — radial or concentric?" (radial)
  "A bullet hole is narrow on the outside of the window and wide on the inside.
  Was the shot fired from outside or inside?" (from outside — the cone widens in
  the direction of travel, so it was traveling inward)

---

### 1:10–1:22 (12 min) — Consolidation

- **[ACTIVITY:]** Note-sheet blocks:
  - **Spatter block**: tail points toward direction of travel; angle =
    arcsin(w/l); round = 90°; velocity table; convergence = draw long axes;
    void/cast-off/arterial.
  - **Glass block**: radial first (opposite side / tension), concentric second
    (force side); 3R rule; cone widens toward travel direction; crack stops at an
    older crack → older one first; fast = clean hole, slow = shattered.

---

### 1:22–1:30 (8 min) — Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket

- **Exit ticket:**
  1. **A blood stain is 4 mm wide and 8 mm long. What is the angle of impact?**
     *(arcsin(4/8) = arcsin 0.5 = 30°)*
  2. **Which glass cracks form first, and on which side of the force?** *(Radial
     cracks form first, on the side opposite the force / the reverse side.)*
  3. **A window has a cone-shaped hole, narrow outside and wide inside. Which
     direction did the projectile travel?** *(From outside to inside — the cone
     widens in the direction of travel.)*

- **Homework:** Homework 11 — paper track (spatter angle calculations + glass
  fracture sequencing). **No home lab** — no spatter or glass at home.

## Coach Notes & Common Kid Mistakes

- **Calculator not in degrees / no arcsin.** Check every calculator *before* the
  session. If it shows the answer in radians, switch the mode; 30° in radians is
  0.52 — a giveaway something's wrong.
- **Swapping width and length.** Width is the **short** axis, length is the
  **long** axis. width ÷ length is always ≤ 1, so arcsin works. If they get a
  ratio > 1, they flipped it.
- **Thinking a bigger drop = more force.** Opposite. Big drops = passive/low
  velocity (gravity). Fine mist = high velocity (gunshot).
- **Tail points backward.** The **pointed tail points in the direction of
  travel** (and satellites spray forward). Kids often say the opposite.
- **Radial vs. concentric mixed up.** Radial = **rays/spokes** = **first**.
  Concentric = **circles** = second. "Radial rays race out first."
- **Cone direction.** The hole is **wider on the exit side** — the cone opens in
  the direction the projectile was going.
- **Sequencing backward.** The crack that **stops** at another crack is the
  **newer** one; the crack it stopped at is **older**.
